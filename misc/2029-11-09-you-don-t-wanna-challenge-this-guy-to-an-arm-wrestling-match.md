# You Don't Wanna Challenge This Guy to an Arm Wrestling Match

AN ARM-WRESTLING match actually begins a few minutes before the referee shouts, “Go!” with a process akin to a very technical, very adversarial handshake. Both arm wrestlers, also called “pullers,” stand with their elbows pressing into pads on a table as the referee arranges and rearranges their palms and fingers, barking reprimands when, say, one puller’s shoulders are not squared or the other’s elbow leaves the pad. The position of the hand is crucial: If one competitor’s webbing (the space between the thumb and index finger) is positioned higher than the other’s, it could confer an advantage.

At a match in front of about 100 spectators in Istanbul in November, two pullers’ right hands are clasped in the middle of a table. As a referee kneads their fingers into place and forces their wrists straight, their nonfighting arms are tense. Their left hands grab at metal pegs on either side of the table. One of the wrestlers, his white hair cut squarely around his face, breathes heavily in and out. His left shoulder is tensed and raised to his ear, and the two prominent veins winding up his arms are already bulging like ranges on a topographic map. His name is John Brzenk, and he is a 59-year-old American—“universally known and recognized as the greatest arm wrestler of all time,” one commentator says as he approaches the table—who has been pulling for more than four decades.

This supermatch, available on pay-per-view from Core Sports, is part of a series called East vs West, and Brzenk’s opponent, 26-year-old Sasho Andreev, is from Bulgaria. Andreev is shorter than Brzenk—five-foot-six to his six-two—but he’s densely built, his arms are comically long, and his hands are the size of salad plates and very thick. Typically, as Brzenk explained to me two weeks prior, pullers with stockier builds excel at “the hook,” a technique that involves rotating and pulling the wrist inward toward the body, drawing the biceps and shoulder into the fight. But Andreev’s Gumby arms—“Look how long his arms are compared to his body,” one of the two commentators exclaims—make him just as capable of executing a top roll, a move in which the wrist curls, then pronates, around the opponent’s hand. Brzenk, too, is effective at both maneuvers.

When the referee starts the bout—releasing their hands, shouting, “Go!” and leaping back in the same split second—the men’s arms are already pushing against each other like two 18-wheelers locking horns. Their faces contort. Brzenk secures a top roll and leans to his left, using his bodyweight to draw Andreev’s hand toward the table, but Andreev has him in a hook—his wrist is straight and Brzenk’s is bent and weakened. Brzenk’s upper lip puffs out, and the vein at his hairline throbs.

“If this goes into an endurance battle, we know the Bulgarian has power there,” one of the commentators says. Both are barely suppressing a bias for the sport’s longtime hero. This match has ceased to be East versus West. It is, instead, age 59 versus 26, and every spectator leans in, wondering if John Brzenk really can—in a high-intensity, strength-based sport—seize a middleweight world title from a man more than 30 years his junior.

“When you talk about age in the sport of arm wrestling, John Brzenk is absolutely trailblazing right now,” Devon Larratt, a top Canadian puller, told me a week before the match. If Brzenk takes the title at 59, Larratt said, “it’s gonna send ripples not just through the arm wrestling community, but I think through the entire fitness and strength community.” Brzenk’s age is not as eye-widening in arm wrestling as it would be in other sports. Nor is Brzenk 59 in the way we have historically understood 59. Other competitors look up to him as a legend who has been shaping modern arm wrestling since 1987, when the film Over the Top, in which Sylvester Stallone plays a puller, came out. The year before, Brzenk had won a tournament held by the filmmakers; he briefly appears in Over the Top—which used footage from the actual tournament—and he took home the tractor trailer Stallone’s character wins. Brzenk was basically undefeated for more than two decades until 2004, has won more than 500 tournaments, and was the middleweight and heavyweight world champion at various times. Most legends step back from their sport after their most competitive years, gazing proudly at the next generation of athletes, like Stallone in Creed cheering from a window while Michael B. Jordan shadowboxes in the street below. Brzenk considered that route and even retired in 2015. But his time in the pasture was brief. He returned to arm wrestling in 2021, when a young puller from Florida, Chance Shaw, challenged him. Brzenk beat Shaw and recommitted to the sport, and he now regularly trounces the next generation on the table.

Arm wrestling is the opposite of aqua jogging: Matches are incredibly demanding on the body, and pullers sometimes even break bones. (Brzenk explains that most breaks happen to those who are newer to the sport: They’re muscular enough to have the torque to hold their position, but their bones have not yet been strengthened by decades of practice. He broke his arm once, at 13, and has not broken it again since.) Yet many of the top competitors are in their 40s and 50s and don’t feel like they’ve peaked by any stretch. Why not?

ON A SATURDAY in late October, as Brzenk is tapering his training ahead of East vs West, I arrive at his home in a peaceful gated community in Mesa, Arizona. I should specify that Brzenk is tapering his training more than usual, because his typical routine is already fairly light. He practices only once a week—earlier in the day, he and several friends who arm wrestle competed on the patio for a few hours—and he spends the other days recovering and doing occasional arm exercises on a pulley system that overlooks his pool.

Now he is sitting in his kitchen, which evokes a Starbucks: It’s painted a warm brown, there is ample seating along two granite islands, and one wall is decorated with a cluster of coffee words in metallic cursive. There is nothing to suggest that this is the lair of a man who could fling you over a table with one hand except for the hand itself, which is holding a mug from the Grand Canyon instructing readers to “Have a Grand Day.”

By trade, Brzenk is a mechanic for Delta Air Lines. Recently, he explains, pullers such as Larratt have been able to make a living from the sport—through their presence on social media more than winnings—but despite the promise of the initial windfall Brzenk received after he won the Over the Top tournament (the tractor trailer was worth $250,000), arm wrestling never became his only source of income. Working for an airline is also fantastic for a puller, because he’s able to fly to far-flung tournaments for free. His humility in the arena suggests he is a pleasant coworker. I imagine he’s Delta’s go-to when one needs a stuck jar opened.

Both of Brzenk’s arms are imposing and vascular: The ridgelike vein that runs up his right forearm and biceps is mirrored on the left. But the right is actually larger, he tells me. The difference used to be more dramatic: In his 20s and 30s, his right forearm was 16 inches in circumference and his left was 12; now the difference is about an inch. As we speak in his kitchen, his right side is angled toward me and he gesticulates more with that arm.

He has never been particularly competitive with his left arm—“I could probably beat 80 percent or 90 percent, but I’m so used to beating more than that,” he says with a smile—chiefly because early in his career there were no opportunities to compete with that arm. Instead, he has done what he says Devon Larratt, who began lifting with and training only his dominant arm during the pandemic, calls “the way of the giant pumpkin”: If you want to grow the biggest pumpkin in all the land, the analogy goes, you should focus on nurturing only that one pumpkin.

Brzenk does not spend much time actively nurturing, however, except for the occasional training he does at the wall-mounted cable station on his patio. When he demonstrates this, grasping a rolling handle attached to 200-pound weights by pulleys and drawing it toward him—all while keeping the handle perfectly stationary—it seems to require zero effort. But he’s found that the low-resistance, downward-pulling exercise builds hand and wrist strength better than traditional lifting. Other than that, he doesn’t lift, and he avoids gyms.

Brzenk readily volunteers that genetics have likely contributed to his success in the sport—most pullers train more often and more heavily. His father was an arm wrestler, and his brother, who started arm wrestling in his early 20s, won several world championships. He recognizes that his tendons, his bone structure, and the size of his forearm, wrist, and hand are uniquely well suited to arm wrestling. (When I ask Michael Todd, another top puller in his 50s, what makes Brzenk so special, he immediately cites his “massive thumb.”)

But there are other factors that catalyze a four-decade career—after all, elite athletes in any sport have primo genes, yet not all compete into their late 50s. Except for the few years he spent in retirement, Brzenk has been devoted to arm wrestling since he was a child. This happens to be a golden age for athletes in their golden years. The role of recovery in ensuring a long career is better understood than ever, and in a sport such as arm wrestling, which is in its infancy—relative to Olympic sports, for instance—and which according to Brzenk rarely involves testing for pharmaceuticals, there is a suite of tools available for an older athlete hoping to stay competitive.

Brzenk is open about his use of testosterone and stem cells, as are other arm wrestlers. He says he didn’t use performance-enhancing drugs during the first few decades of his career, but a doctor prescribed him AndroGel (a topical testosterone-replacement medication), then a bit of testosterone, in his 40s, when he noticed he was losing mass. “I don’t want to hurt myself, but if you’ve got the fountain of youth, show me where it is,” he says, laughing. “If I can stay healthy and continue to do what I want to do at a high level and be super healthy with it, sign me up.” The impact of these tools on the sport, says Larratt, who calls stem cells a “lifesaver,” is huge: “If the trends continue, you’re gonna have world champions who are 100. And that would be completely impossible decades ago. That’s medical.”

Brad Schoenfeld, Ph.D., C.S.C.S., a Men’s Health exercise-science advisor, says that pharmacological aids really can offset the consequences of the hormonal declines we experience with age—as long as, he qualifies, an athlete has good genes and good lifestyle habits. In practice, this means that a 30-year-old arm wrestler could face off (arm off?) against someone who is nearly as strong as him but who has three decades more experience.

Several particularities of arm wrestling also make it more suitable for older athletes than, say, gymnastics. The specialized strength that Brzenk and Larratt build, for example, is relatively easy to keep up, says orthopedic surgeon Chris Raynor, M.D., who interviewed Larratt on his YouTube channel, Not Your Everyday Ortho, last spring. “Rather than having to maintain the entire body, you’re only having to maintain a portion of the body,” Dr. Raynor says. As the demands of middle age seep into athletes’ lives, it becomes more difficult to find time for daily whole-body workouts; focusing your energy on one arm is more sustainable.

Dr. Raynor also points out that many older arm wrestlers have been consistently subjecting their bones and tendons to resistance for a very long time. While most of us will suffer a decline in the structure and strength of our joints, arm wrestlers may counteract that with frequent exposure to force (heavy force in matches and gentle force on patio pulleys). In addition, he says, “these guys, with a lifetime of experience, are just smarter. They know how to train harder; they know how to perform better, with less, and in a manner that is less likely to get them injured.”

Brian Feeley, M.D., chief of sports medicine and shoulder surgery at the University of California, San Francisco, tells me that endurance athletes can be limited by a decline in their peak heart rate, which occurs with aging. “It’s going to be harder to run the same speed you did when you were 25 for 45 minutes, but your strength can still stay up there,” he says. If you watch a marathon, Dr. Feeley adds, though you’ll see a lot of young people charging through it, you’re also likely to see many runners in their 40s and 50s, even their 60s, doing pretty well. “Obviously, they trained a lot. It’s not like every 52-year-old can get up and run a marathon. But whereas you lose that burst, if you maintain that muscle, that means you have a lot of those stamina-type muscle fibers that are going to allow for more sustained contractions.”

Brzenk has noticed that while he used to be better able to “sprint” in matches, starting strong and ferocious, now he has better stamina. When I mention this to Dr. Feeley, he brings up golf. Golfers in their 50s may not have the same fast-twitch power they had in their 20s, he says, but their skill has increased. “In a lot of these skill-based sports, you can still be really, really good.” Brzenk, he explains, has likely “transitioned from that fast twitch to a skill, endurance-based program, and he’s probably smarter and more savvy than most of the other people out there.”

AGE COMES WITH unavoidable limitations, however—chiefly, for Brzenk, the moment when a movement causes the body to say nope. He didn’t always listen. When he was younger, he often pushed through the pain. Now he likes to say his career is primarily about survival—not injuring himself, not hurting anybody else, and trying to enjoy it. He has to work to balance his ambition with his limitations: When he feels a quick twang in his arm as he practices, he doesn’t push through it like he used to. “It’s definitely easy to want to be what I once was, and my mind puts me on a course that could potentially cause me to hurt myself. Yeah. So it’s . . .” He trails off, then smiles wryly. “That’s not the winning attitude. You can’t be just trying to survive. . . . When it comes tournament time, I’m completely like, No, if it has to break, it has to break. I will just lay it all out on the line.”

When I ask him whether he is competitive outside of arm wrestling, he looks at his wife, Renée, who has perched on a kitchen stool. They met when they were growing up in McHenry, Illinois. “Am I competitive?” he asks her, in an innocent voice common among board-game terrorists. “I guess I am competitive.”

“Yes, you are,” Renée says. “You usually always win at everything you do.”

“But I’m okay with losing sometimes. It’s hard to explain. I care, but I don’t care. I don’t get too caught up.”

Losing occasionally is the tax you pay to continue competing into what is, for many, a stage of life characterized by sweatpants and sloth, and Brzenk allows that accepting those losses has been difficult for him. “I had so much success for so many years. Not really necessarily undefeated, but 99 percent of the times I would arm wrestle, I would win,” he says. But what, he asks, is the alternative? “Be afraid to lose and don’t ever put yourself out there and don’t compete? Or be able to mentally accept it and say: Sometimes you’re going to win, sometimes you’re going to lose, and you are gonna be 60 this year; it’s okay to not be the best.”

But in Istanbul in November, as a surge of strength from Sasho Andreev in the first round sends Brzenk listing to the right, the primal muscle memory honed over four decades of arm wrestling kicks in. Andreev’s torso is parallel to the table as he tries to pull his opponent down, but Brzenk holds fast, shaking. Then, like a rubber band reaching the end of its stretch and going slack, Brzenk lets the back of his hand touch the pad on the table. Breathing hard, Brzenk looks up as the referee unwinds the strap from their hands.

He has lost the first round, but the commentators are still impressed. “The thing you need to understand is: John Brzenk just stopped one of the most dominant champions in WAF [World Arm Wrestling Federation] history,” says a commentator. “Sasho Andreev usually just needs two surges to finish an opponent. John Brzenk, at 59 years old, went toe-to-toe with him, and I believe he still has something in him left,” one says. Brzenk, grinning wide and still catching his breath, approaches the table again.

He loses two more rounds, and the match, his fortitude diminishing with each sudden push from Andreev. After the third loss, he raises his hands over the table in an all-hail motion, smiling and dripping sweat. The match could end now, but Brzenk agrees to another round. He loses that one, too, and looks utterly depleted. But he returns to the table one more time and slaps it twice with his palm: again. After his fifth loss, Brzenk, laughing, reaches his left arm across the table and pats the shoulder of Andreev, who draws him into an embrace.

Brzenk told me in October that he makes a point of being gracious in victory and defeat. “I think most people want that confirmation from the other athlete: You were formidable,” he says, cheerfully articulating the word. “Even if you lose—if the guy kills you—he’s still looking for the acknowledgment. Like, you belong here.”

In the postgame press conference, Andreev remarks, in Bulgarian, that he’s in the best shape of his career but that the resistance from Brzenk stunned him. Perhaps this is the key to competing in one’s leisure years: learning that it’s okay not to be the best, as long as you’re formidable.
