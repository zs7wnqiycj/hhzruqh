# Maingaining Takes Next-Level Focus and Commitment in the Gym. This Workout Provides the Plan.

IF YOUR FITNESS goal is to look more muscular, we’ve got the playbook. And instead of the old-school “eat big to get big” bulking approach that’s dominated training circles for decades, this plan takes a different route. The idea is to build muscle at a steadier pace to minimize (or even eliminate) the extra fat gain that usually comes with bulking. This approach to muscle gain has become a recent trend called “maingaining.”

The maingaining plan requires focus and discipline in the gym and the kitchen. You'll need to be calculated in your calorie intake while dedicating time and effort to your workouts. There is some promising research that backs it up; one small study out of the Auckland University of Technology suggested that a small bump in calories can deliver similar strength and hypertrophy results to the traditional bulk-cut cycle—with fewer unwanted pounds.

If you're already in decent shape, this will be a particularly good plan for you. "In my opinion, this is one of the most sustainable long-term ways you can train because you don't need to shift your diet a ton," explains Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S., fitness director of Men’s Health and the creator of this program. "You can keep getting stronger, keep building muscle. You don't need to put on a bunch of fat."

Samuel’s approach is grounded in two basic principles: train hard and eat smart. You’ll hit the weights four to five days per week and push with intent for the next two months. You’ll eat in a slight surplus to fuel performance, but remember your diet isn’t a free-for-all. Yes, you can gain muscle by crushing calories, but you’ll also end up having to cut for many months to realize your progress.

Keep the surplus tight, train aggressively, and stay consistent. That’s the ethos. For more guidance, check out the entire Men's Health Ultimate Maingaining program.

What You Need to Do for Maingaining

Eat in a Slight Caloric Surplus

To gain muscle, you must eat more calories than you burn every day. The difference here is just how much more. During a typical bulk, you’d eat about 500 extra calories to gain a pound per week. For this plan, however, Samuel recommends that you eat 100 to 200 calories (roughly equal to one scoop of protein powder in water) above your maintenance level, which you can estimate simply using this tool. The muscle gains will be slower, but the idea is you’ll slowly gain muscle tissue with little to no noticeable fat gain. You can check out our nutrition guide for more information on how to eat while following this program.

Walk 8,000 to 10,000 Steps Per Day

Consistently walking can help you lose or maintain weight, aid in muscle recovery, and improve your heart health. Samuel suggests aiming for 8,000 to 10,000 steps daily: “It's also going to give you good blood flow, and especially when it comes to steps, it's going to help you recover from the hard workouts overall,” he says.

Understand Progression

If you’re lifting the same weights week to week, your gains aren’t going to budge, which is why Samuel hammers home the importance of progressing your workouts every single session.

“That is the best way to build muscle and strength in the gym, but you need it that much more when you don't have a ton of extra calories,” says Samuel. “We just have to be smart about how we're thinking about progressive overload.”

How to Progress This Workout

Week 1

This week is about acclimating to the exercises in the program. Take your time performing warm-up sets for every exercise to find a weight you can push yourself with. Once you’re ready for the “working” sets, aim for two reps in reserve (check out this example to see how that works in practice).

Week 2

Your goal this week is to progress every lift a little bit, so you reach one rep in reserve. If you hit the lower end of each rep range during week one, then add a rep to your sets. Or increase the weight by as few pounds as your gym’s weights allow and try to hit the same number of reps as last week.

Weeks 3 to 5

Samuel wants you to reach technical failure for the final set of your first exercise on your heavy days—meaning you can’t do any rep with good form. Your technique, not your muscles, should break down. For heavy barbell exercises, make sure you're working with a spotter whenever possible. For every other exercise, keep one to two reps in the tank, and lift heavy enough so you’re in the bottom-end of the prescribed rep range; the sets should still feel challenging.

Weeks 4 to 6

Time to push it. Aim to reach technical failure for the final two sets of your first exercise on your heavy days. Samuel wants you to add one drop-set to an exercise of your choosing on your volume days. This is a good opportunity to target a muscle that lags in size or strength with extra volume. For every other exercise, hit one RIR.

Weeks 6 to 8

Return to the week one protocol of two reps in reserve for every exercise. By this point, you should be stronger; try to increase the weight on every movement to still hit two RIR.

Crucial Components

Warm Up With Intention

Over the years, well-meaning influencers have overcomplicated the idea of a warm-up, turning it into a 20-minute sequence of mobility and activation drills. You don’t need all of that. Before every workout, you’ll do two stretches that’ll cover you from head to toe:

Step your left leg forward so it’s alongside your left hand and your knee forms a 90-degree angle. Squeeze your right glute as you do this, and hold for a second.

Keeping your left hand on the ground, reach your right hand toward the ceiling, continuing to keep your hips square. Your eyes should follow your right hand.

Reverse the movements back to pushup position, then repeat on the other side.

From there, perform a few ramp-up sets for each exercise, gradually increasing the load until you reach your working weight. How many sets you need depends on how heavy you’re lifting. Hammer curls may only require one ramp-up set, while trap bar deadlifts might need three or four. This simple approach raises your body temperature, gets your joints moving, and prepares your muscles to produce force.

Keep Your Form Tight

This program asks you to train with intensity—a requirement for building muscle—so resist the urge to half-rep or swing your way through sets. Perform every lift with textbook form: lower the weight under control, move through a full range of motion, and keep constant tension on the target muscles.

You’ll gauge your reps in reserve using technical failure, which means a set is over once you can no longer complete a rep with good technique.

Stick to the Rest Times

The listed rest periods are not suggestions—they’re a crucial part of the program. Rest too little between heavy sets, and you’ll short-change your strength; take too long of a rest between lighter sets, and you’ll go cold and lose out on the conditioning aspect of the exercise.

The first two exercises for each workout demand heavier weights, so rest two to three minutes between sets to ensure you’re fully rested. For all other exercises, take a minute to a minute-and-a-half to recover. Use a timer, start your next set with purpose, and let the structure do its job.

Workouts

Lower Body Heavy

Trap Bar Deadlift

WHY: The trap bar deadlift is beginner-friendly and ideal for overloading with heavier weight. The high handles make it easier to achieve an optimal starting position, while the centered load reduces lumbar stress and allows you to pull with more power and control than a traditional barbell deadlift.

SETS AND REPS: 4 sets of 4 to 6 reps

HOW TO DO IT:

Position yourself inside the trap bar, with your shins aligned with (or just in front of) the center of the bar.

Push your butt back as far as possible, bend your knees, and reach down to grip the handles. Grip as tightly as possible.

Keep your head in a neutral position, maintaining a fixed gaze at something in front of you. Squeeze your shoulder blades to create tension, and turn the pits of your elbows forward, facing out.

Make sure your hips are lower than your shoulders, then prepare to initiate the lift.

Push your feet through the floor to stand straight up, squeezing your glutes at the top.

To finish the rep, push your butt back as far as you can, then bend your knees to set the weight down.

Deficit Reverse Lunge

WHY: The reverse lunge targets the glutes and quads while challenging the stabilizers of the hips and core. Elevating the front foot increases the range of motion and stretch on the lead leg, promoting greater muscle growth while reinforcing lower body control and mobility.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 6 to 8 reps each side

Start with both feet up on the platform.

Lift one foot up and off the platform, stepping back behind you into a rear lunge.

Allow the back knee to come as close to the floor as possible.

Goblet Squat + Kettlebell Swing

WHY: Goblet squats are absolute quad torchers, delivering a brutal leg pump while reinforcing clean squat mechanics. Kettlebell swings shifts the focus to explosive hip extension, lighting up the glutes and hamstrings for a balanced lower body stimulus.

SETS AND REPS: 10-minute AMRAP

HOW TO DO THE GOBLET SQUAT:

Stand with your feet just wider than shoulder-width apart. Turn your toes out slightly to start; as you progress, find the most comfortable stance for your own mobility.

Hold a kettlebell or dumbbell with both at chest-height, elbows high. Crate mid-back tension to pull your shoulders back. Brace your core to keep your balance; you'll need to maintain this tension throughout the movement.

Push your butt back, then bend your knees to descend down into the squat. Lower down to a depth just below parallel (i.e., your thighs are parallel to the floor) or to the most comfortable position given your personal mobility.

Press your knees apart to prevent them from caving in. Maintain tension in your core and shoulders; don't rest your elbows on your knees.

Push off the floor and squeeze your glutes to stand back up.

HOW TO DO THE KETTLEBELL SWING:

Start standing with your feet slightly wider than hip-width apart, with the kettlebell on the ground in front of you.

Push you butt back, then lean over to grip the bell's handle. Hold on tightly.

Squeeze your shoulder blades, then shift your arms to turn the pits of your elbows outward. Squeeze your abs to create tension. Keep your eyes on the floor to maintain a neutral spine.

"Hike" the weight back between your legs, keeping your knees slightly bent. Keep your arms straight.

Stand straight up, locking your knees, and aggressively squeeze your glutes to perform the swing. Keep your arms loose, like ropes connecting yourself to the kettlebell. Don't aim for a certain point; allow the momentum to determine how high the bell goes.

Allow the weight to fall back down, back through your legs.

Upper Body Volume

Seal or Machine Row

WHY: Whether you choose a seal row or a chest-supported machine row, the benefit is the same: taking momentum out of the lift and placing the workload squarely on your back. With your torso supported, these rows allow you to train the upper back hard, exactly what you want when the goal is serious size.

SETS AND REPS: 4 sets of 8 to 10 reps

To perform a seal row, you'll need an elevated flat bench positioned high enough so that you can lie chest-down on the pad and extend your arms without touching the floor. You can use weight plates or some other platform to raise the bench; just make sure it's stable enough to hold your weight.

Lie on the bench, hanging your arms straight down holding dumbbells in a neutral grip or a barbell with an overhand grip. Squeeze your shoulder blades, abs, and glutes to create full-body tension.

Raise the weight up to bench height, pausing to squeeze your mid-back muscles at the top. Fight to keep your legs still.

Lower the weight under control back to the starting position.

Incline Alternating Dumbbell Press

WHY: This variation emphasizes the upper chest and shoulders while increasing core demand, since pressing one dumbbell at a time forces you to resist rotation and maintain stability. It’s an efficient way to build pressing strength and upper body mass without simply piling on more weight.

Sit on the incline bench set to a 45 to 60 degree angle holding a set of dumbbells. Drive your feet into the floor, drive your glutes into the seat, squeeze your abs, and drive your shoulders into the back pad.

Raise the dumbbells to your shoulders. If the weight is too heavy, use your knees to kick the bell up. Your forearms should be perpendicular to the floor.

Press the weight straight up, keeping your upper arms perpendicular to the ground.

Once the weight is up, drive your shoulders down to create tension. Shift the angle of your arms by turning your hands slightly in.

Lower one arm down toward your chest as your flexibility allows, at least until your elbow is below your shoulder. Keep your opposite arm extended overhead, squeezing your abs to stay balanced on the bench.

Press the weight back up, then repeat with the opposite arm. Continue alternating until you've performed the prescribed reps.

Tri-Set: Lat Pulldown + Half-Kneeling Overhead Press + Paused Lateral Raise

WHY: This tidy tri-set condenses the rest of your upper-body work into back and shoulder movements, and building size on these muscles enhances the V-taper that’s synonymous with a jacked physique. Also, half-kneeling press and paused raises reinforce control and shoulder stability as fatigue sets in.

SETS AND REPS: 3 rounds of 8 to 10 reps

HOW TO DO THE LAT PULLDOWN:

Set seat at a comfortable height. Grab the handle using an overhand grip with your hands at about shoulder width.

Focus on keeping your feet planted into the ground, core tight, and maintain a tight grip on the bar.

Lean back slightly—do not arch your back for this—then, squeezing your shoulder blades, drive them down as you pull. Remember to drive your elbows down as well as you pull the bar to the top of your chest.

Hold momentarily at the bottom, then return to start position under control.

HOW TO DO THE HALF-KNEELING OVERHEAD PRESS:

Get into a half-kneeling position on the ground, holding kettlebells or dumbbells in each hand. Squeeze your core and glutes to make it a strong position.

Raise both weights into the rack position, holding the handles and resting the weights on your forearms if you use kettlebells.

Press both weights straight up.

Lower back down to the starting position under control.

HOW TO DO THE STANDING PAUSED LATERAL RAISE:

Start holding a pair of light dumbbells at your sides in a neutral position. Stand with a slight bend in your knees, squeezing your shoulder blades, abs, and glutes to create tension. Keep your shoulder blades rolled down and back, rather than shrugging upwards ahead of your reps.

Shift the dumbbells slightly in front of your torso.

Lift the dumbbells upwards. As you raise the weights, rotate the dumbbell slightly up to shift your shoulders into external rotation.

Raise until the weights are just below shoulder height (if standing with your arms extended straight out from your torso is a 90 degree position, raise up to 89 degrees).

Pause at the top position.

Lower the weight back down to the starting position under control.

Cable Crunch

WHY: You still need to lose body fat to reveal your abs—but directly training the rectus abdominis (the “six-pack” muscle) can make them appear thicker and more pronounced once you lean out. The cable crunch earns a top spot because it’s simple to learn, easy to control, and allows for progressive loading, delivering a high stimulus to the abs without unnecessary complexity.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 10 to 12 reps

Set the pulley of a cable machine to its highest setting and attach a rope handle.

Select the weight you want to use and then grab the rope with both hands and drop to your knees.

Scoot back a few inches, bring the rope near the top of your head, and then crunch your head toward your hips. Don't shoot your butt back; think about pushing your belly button to the ceiling and rounding your back.

Optional Arm Day

Cable Curl + Close-Grip Push-Up

WHY: This superset sets the tone for a hard, heavy arm workout. Cable curls maintain constant tension through the full range of motion, forcing the biceps to work from start to finish, while close-grip push-ups overload the triceps—especially in the stretched position at the bottom of each rep.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps

HOW TO DO THE CABLE CURL:

Grab the handle with one hand and take a step and a half away from the machine (or as far as you need to feel tension on the muscle).

Squeeze the abs and glutes for a solid base.

Create a slight angle with the elbow to where the cable feels like it's pulling you back.

Ensure that your hips and shoulders are facing square in front—don't allow the tension from the cable to twist your torso backwards.

Moving only at the elbow, curl your hand towards your shoulder.

As you lower back down, make sure the cable doesn't pull your shoulder from its position. Think about keeping the upper arm in line with your torso.

HOW TO DO THE CLOSE-GRIP PUSH-UP:

Start in a high plank position as you would for a standard pushup, with your palms flat on the floor at about shoulder-width and your weight resting on your toes. Squeeze your shoulder blades, abs, and glutes to create tension, and keep your gaze on the floor to keep your neck neutral.

Shift your hands in closer together, just narrower than shoulder-width. Turn the pits of your elbows forward to activate your lats.

Press up off the floor and totally extend your arms at the top of the rep.

Skull Crusher + Hammer Curl

WHY: Skull crushers load the triceps in a deep stretch, which is a proven way to drive muscle growth when you train with intent. Use dumbbells here, either on a flat bench or on the floor. Pair them with hammer curls to bring the forearms into play—because bigger arms aren’t just about your biceps.

HOW TO DO THE SKULL CRUSHER:

Start by lying back on the bench, holding a pair of dumbbells in a neutral grip. Don't arch your back—drive your shoulder blades into the bench, squeeze your abs and glutes, and keep your feet flat on the floor.

Press the weight straight up above your chest. Your upper arm should be just past 90 degrees, at a 91 or 92 degree angle. Keep your wrists strong.

Lower the weights slowly down on either side of your head, moving only at the elbows. Don't allow your shoulders to shift forward; keep your upper arm still.

Drive the weight back up (again moving only at the elbows), squeezing your triceps at the top.

HOW TO DO THE HAMMER CURL:

Stand with your feet hip-width apart, holding a pair of dumbbells in a neutral grip (palms facing each other). Squeeze your shoulder blades, abs, and glutes to create full-body tension.

Curl the dumbbell up, moving only at the elbow joint. Keep your upper arms still and perpendicular to the floor. Squeeze your biceps at the top of the movement.

Avoid shifting your elbow forward to keep your shoulders out of the movement.

Spider Curl + Pushdown

WHY: Spider curls lock your upper arms in place, taking momentum out of the equation and forcing the biceps to work hard at the top of the rep where they’re fully shortened. Then you’ll keep constant tension on the triceps with pushdowns, making this superset an efficient way to finish off your arms.

Lower Body Volume

Paused Romanian Deadlift

WHY: Romanian deadlifts deliver a deep stretch to the hamstrings while still allowing you to move serious weight. Adding a pause at the bottom of each rep increases time under tension and reinforces control, making this one of the most effective exercises you can use to build hamstring size and strength.

Start by getting the weight into place. If you're using a barbell, you can load the bar onto a rack just below hip-level, grab the weight using an pronated (overhand) grip, then stand holding the bar. With dumbbells, grab them from a bench or box, then hold with a neutral grip at your sides.

Organize your body by squeezing your shoulder blades, abs, and glutes. With a barbell, turn on your lats by "breaking the bar"—turn your armpits forward while gripping the barbell hard.

Push your butt back, then hinge at the hips to lower your torso. Keep your spine and neck in a neutral position by looking at the ground.

Lower down until you feel tension in your hamstrings.

Walking Pendulum Lunge

WHY: This continuous lunge variation has you perform a reverse lunge into a forward lunge as one repetition. It is a brutal combination of quad, glute, and stabilizer work that drives muscle growth, coordination, and conditioning all at once.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps (each leg)

Stand with your feet shoulder-width apart. Squeeze your glutes, and, and shoulder blades, keeping your gaze neutral at a point straight ahead of you.

Step one leg back and slightly out, landing with your toe first. Don't slam your knee into the ground. Keep your chest in an upright position, bending your knees to form right angles with both of your legs.

Drive off the ground with your front foot and step your rear leg directly into a forward lunge. Keep your torso in a solid upright position by squeezing your core to stay balanced.

Repeat with the opposite leg to continue moving forward.

Tri-Set: Alternating Lateral Lunge + Zercher Heels-Elevated Half-Full Squat + Staggered-Stance Kettlebell Swing

WHY: This lower-body tri-set hits your legs from every angle. Lateral lunges build strength and mobility in the frontal (side-to-side) plane, the heels-elevated Zercher squat keeps the quads under constant tension through a challenging range of motion, and staggered-stance swings finish things off by hammering the glutes and hamstrings with explosive hip drive.

SETS AND REPS: 3 rounds of 8 to 10 reps per exercise

HOW TO DO THE ALTERNATING LATERAL LUNGE:

Start standing, holding a kettlebell or dumbbell at your chest, core tight.

Step to the right a few feet with your right leg, taking a relatively large step.

Land. Keeping your left leg straight, bend your right knee and push your butt back, lowering slowly.

Lower as far as you can comfortably, aiming to get thigh parallel to the ground.

Then explosively drive up and to the left, driving back to a standing position.

Repeat with the left leg.

HOW TO DO THE ZERCHER HEELS-ELEVATED HALF-FULL SQUAT:

Start holding a dumbbell or kettlebell in goblet position or a barbell in Zercher position, with your feels elevated on a weight plate or some other stable platform. Focus on squeezing your shoulder blades to create mid-back tension, along with your abs and glutes.

Push your butt back and bend your knees to lower down into the squat, lowering to the bottom point of what your mobility allows. Fight to keep your torso upright as the weight pulls you forward. Hold that bottom position for a second or two, but don't rest your elbows on your knees.

Push up off the floor, standing until you hit the halfway point. Pause, then lower back down to the bottom position.

Stand up to finish the full rep, squeezing your glutes to extend your hips.

HOW TO DO THE STAGGERED STANCE KETTLEBELL SWING:

Alternating Gorilla Row

WHY: The gorilla row is a heavy, no-frills back builder that hits the lats, upper back, and grip hard. Alternating reps forces your core to resist rotation while allowing you to generate more force by bracing against the grounded kettlebell. As you row, drive one arm into the top of the bell while pulling the other toward your hip.

SETS AND REPS: 3 sets of 6 to 8 reps

Stand in a wide stance with a pair of kettlebells on the ground between your legs. Push your butt back and squeeze your glutes and hamstrings, reaching down to grab the weights. Make sure your shoulders are higher than your butt.

Squeeze your shoulder blade and row one bell toward your rib cage. Pause briefly at the top. Drive down into the floor with the non-working hand, fighting to avoid torso rotation.

Lower the weight back down to the floor under control.

Repeat with the other arm.

Push Press

WHY: The push press lets you move heavier weight overhead by using leg drive, making it a powerful tool to overload your shoulders and build explosiveness in the upper body. It bridges the gap between pure strength and explosive power.

Start in a standard military press position, holding a barbell or dumbbells in both hands at shoulder height.

Dip your hips and knees slightly and explode upwards, even possibly rising onto your toes.

Use that upward energy to help propel the weight overhead. Make sure you keep your abs and glutes tight as you do this.

Lower back down under control, taking three to four seconds.
