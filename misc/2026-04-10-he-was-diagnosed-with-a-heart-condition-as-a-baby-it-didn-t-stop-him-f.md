# He Was Diagnosed With a Heart Condition as a Baby. It Didn’t Stop Him From Winning 3 Olympic Golds.

HERE'S A SOBERING fact: Every 40 seconds, someone in the US has a heart attack, and heart disease still kills more Americans than all cancers and accidents combined. It’s the number one killer of both men and women—but many heart conditions are totally treatable if they’re caught early.

Case in point: Since he was diagnosed at just one year old, former professional snowboarder and three-time Olympic gold medalist Shaun White didn’t let his congenital heart condition, Tetralogy of Fallot, hold him back.

Similarly, Carly Pearce, a Grammy-winning American country music singer and songwriter, was diagnosed with pericarditis, which is inflammation of the tissue surrounding the heart, at age 29. Today, thanks to the fact that she advocated for and educated herself, the condition is just a part of her life, rather than something that’s taken it over.

The trio is joined by Sunil Rao, MD, a cardiologist from NYU Langone Health, who shares what he’s most excited about when it comes to preventive cardiology in 2025.

Here’s a look at some of the themes covered in the episode:

The early signs that tipped Pearce off that something wasn’t rightA runner since the age of 19, Pearce started noticing symptoms like shortness of breath and stabbing chest pain that led her to seek medical attention. Dr. Rao says these symptoms can also be signs of coronary artery disease, which is the number one cause of heart attack, so it's important to get a full work-up if you experience them.

The scary event that ultimately led to Pearce’s pericarditis diagnosisAfter fainting at dinner, she took a trip to the ER, which led to an EKG and ultimately her diagnosis. Pearce has recurrent pericarditis, and experienced a second “big” flare in 2024 while at a photo shoot.

How Pearce adapted her life post-diagnosis“This is the first time that I was like, you get one heart, and I want to make sure that I take care of it because it's clearly angry at me at times,” Pearce says. Initially, she swapped running for walking and went public with her diagnosis to explain why her shows would look a little different.

The importance of advocating for your own healthIf you're not getting the answers that you want from your care provider, insist on getting those answers, Dr. Rao says. He also encourages patients to seek second opinions. “You know your body better than anybody, better than any doctor,” Pearce adds. “If it is telling you something, listen to it.”

How the narrative around heart conditions is changing“It's really important for people to realize that, for a lot of patients who have heart disease, it's not really anything that they did,” Dr. Rao says. “It's not because you decided to have an extra cheeseburger last month.” Instead, it's part of the human condition and of genetics. “There have been mummies found in ancient Egypt that have coronary artery disease,” he says.

The proactive steps men can be taking to prevent or manage heart issuesDr. Rao points to the American Heart Association’s Life's Essential 8, which includes controlling cholesterol, eating mostly whole foods, and not smoking.

The benefits of all movement for heart health, including walkingEven 20 minutes a few times a week adds up, Dr. Rao says.

The role that sleep plays in heart health“There are associations between lack of good quality sleep and the development of high blood pressure and heart disease,” Dr. Rao says. To optimize sleep quality, he talks about exercise, the timing of your last meal of the day, and even certain supplements.

The screenings that men and women should be getting—and whenDr. Rao says everyone should see their physician once a year starting at age 30 to get their blood pressure and cholesterol checked at a minimum.

White’s message for others confronting heart health conditions today“It's a time to dig deep and to learn about yourself,” he says, adding that eating well and getting regular check-ups are small but powerful and actionable steps people can take.

What Dr. Rao is most excited about in the field of cardiology
