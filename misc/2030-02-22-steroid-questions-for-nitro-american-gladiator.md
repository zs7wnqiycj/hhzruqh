# Steroid Questions for Nitro, American Gladiator

When he wasn’t bashing the brains of weekend warriors with a giant Q-tip or laying vicious hits on spandex-wearing guys in Powerball, Dan Clark—“Nitro” of the original American Gladiators—was slamming needles into his glutes. The former San Jose State linebacker and reality TV megastar abused steroids for more than 2 decades to build his formidable frame.

[Steroid Questions for Nitro](https://pepperedx.com/dglcomb/)

Clark details his steroid use, rise to fame, terrifying bouts of ‘roid rage, and his life after the drugs—including relapses and daily pains—in his frank new book, Gladiator. MensHealth.com got an early look at the manuscript, and the chance to ask Nitro some questions about steroids, their lasting effects, and his workouts today.

MH: When we hear about high-level professional athletes using steroids, they cycle on and off the drugs to avoid detection and protect their natural testosterone production from permanent damage. You don’t mention cycling off in Gladiator—did you avoid this to keep things interesting? Or did you really not cycle off?

Dan Clark/Nitro: I took my first steroid in 1981. It was such a different culture than today. I went to a steroid doctor and got a prescription for the stuff. He told me, ‘Look, you don’t need to go off.’ You stayed on your cycle—I would stay on a cycle for a year. You’d change your drugs to change the stimulus.

Inevitably, as your body stops producing testosterone, you have to take more drugs. It’s an insidious cycle that doesn’t stop. And when you do go off, no matter how much HCG you take—no matter what you take—you’re still at a deficiency. Stuff doesn’t work right anymore. The longer you’re on, the more you’ve destroyed your endocrine system.

It’s similar to other drugs: Eventually, users can’t get dopamine unless they use the drugs, because their body stops producing it.

MH: In the book, you detail a rage-fueled fight with your best friend, Jim Kalafat (“Laser” of AG)—you wound up with scars on your face from his teeth. How much does a bout of “’roid rage” take over your psyche? Were you both completely out of control?

DC: Sometimes, the rage is bigger than you are. It triggers, and the next thing you know, a little argument becomes your hands around your best friend’s neck. I grabbed him; he grabbed me. And we’re going at it.

The thing that bothers me: There are pundits that say there is no such thing as ‘roid rage. That’s a blatant lie. I’ve seen guys pick up guys by their feet for no reason. Not everyone that takes steroids goes out and gets in fistfights, but to say there’s no rage factor—that’s a flat-out, blatant lie.

MH: You outline some of the daily pains you endure today after years of ravaging your body—even walking up and down stairs is painful, and you’re only 44. Can you give us some more detail?

DC: I already had missing cartilage in my right knee—there’s torn cartilage in there, too, now. I have herniated disks in my lower back, and pain in my shoulders, arms, ankles, and fingers.

Put it this way: When I go to the movies with my girlfriend, I can’t just stand up. I have to push with both arms to get up at the end of the movie. I’m not in a wheelchair, but what is it going to be like when I’m 55?

Part of my crusade is finding ways to rejuvenate and alleviate the pain. I’ve spent thousands of dollars on acupuncture and chiropractics. I have a couple hundred anti-inflammatories in my drawer. Taking those since 35—I’m now 44—is horrible. The stuff lives in your kidneys. But I try to find anything I can to restore my body, live a comfortable life, and manage the pain.

MH: When you were on steroids, you claim to have benched 500 pounds for reps. How does Nitro exercise now—9 years after going off the juice?

DC: I still consider myself to be in excellent shape. But I hate going to the gym.

Now, my life is more full. Before, besides the steroids, the only way I knew how to feel good about myself was to get applause or to exercise. That’s also an addiction. When your life is full, you say “I’ve got to go to the gym.” You don’t want to go to the gym.

Exercise is my way to keep my sanity, though—I release toxins, stress, anxiety. I do yoga, pilates, some stationary bike. I do some weight training, but it’s more about cardiovascular health and fitness. For me, variety is the spice of life, and it’s what leads to consistency and longevity. So I go box for 6 weeks. Then I’ll cycle for six. Let me swim for a month. Yoga for a few years.

MH: Early in your steroid use, you indicate that your steroid doctor had you taking the drugs “the right way”—in small, controlled doses. Do you think that if the substances could be controlled, they could be used in a positive way (in small doses) to help improve men’s lives?

DC: I think any time you start talking about what amount you can take, you’re opening a Pandora’s box. Society puts it on us to be bigger, stronger, faster, better. And once you go down that road with steroids … I was never big enough for myself. They become a hopeless reach for happiness.

Even if you tried to restrict it to older men for hormone replacement, there’d be a bleed-down effect: ‘If he’s 50 and can use it, I should be able to use it now.’ It’s a box that should not be opened.

MH: Athletes who used steroids before they were banned—when they were prescribed by doctors—are being called “cheaters” today. You played football (at San Diego State and as a replacement NFL player) during those times—is the “cheater” characterization fair?

DC: Focusing on the past isn’t going to solve the problems of the future. We’re enveloped in this crisis of steroids. We need strict, rigorous testing now, and if there are offenders, we need to punish them swiftly and severely. We need to educate the teenagers who are coming up who may be inclined to use these drugs.
