WHERE YOU TRAIN matters. Working out in a commercial gym, a dim basement, or a garage can be convenient, but you’re probably not going to train consistently if you dread where you workout. And consistency, more than any single piece of home gym equipment, is what keeps your gains moving in the right direction.

That’s part of the appeal with training outside. You get fresh air, sunlight, more space, and the freedom to move without waiting for a weight bench, dodging a tripod, or pretending you enjoy the smell of your local group exercise class. But outdoor workouts don’t have to mean jogging, hiking, or doing a few pushups in the grass and calling it a day. With the right gear, you can build strength, power, and conditioning in your backyard, driveway, garage, or any open patch of space you have access to.

The key is choosing equipment that actually makes sense for the outdoors. That means durable, portable, easy to store, and versatile enough to justify carrying it out of the house in the first place. Below, we’ve rounded up our favorite pieces of outdoor fitness equipment to help you build a more capable, nature-forward training setup without dragging an entire gym into your yard.

Few training tools come close to a weighted sled for building the kind of strength and conditioning that translates to everyday life. Sled work is brutally simple. You push, pull, or drag a load for distance, training your legs, trunk, grip, and lungs in a way that carries over to hauling groceries, pushing a stroller uphill, moving furniture, or dragging yard junk across the lawn.

The roughly $800 price tag makes the TANK M1 a tough sell for most home-gym owners, but the price is partly justified by Torque’s smart design. Most notably, the M1 is an all-surface sled. Instead of metal skis grinding across concrete, it uses wheels and magnetic resistance, so you can push it on concrete, turf, rubber, and other surfaces without needing weight plates to create friction. Your neighbors will appreciate that, too, as it avoids the metal-on-concrete scraping that comes standard with most traditional sleds.

The M1 is available in two packages: the Athlete Kit and the Complete Kit. Both include the sled, console, dumbbell cradles, V-strap, tow strap and harness, and phone holder. The Complete Kit adds wheelbarrow handles for more carry-style conditioning and a wall-mounted storage system to save garage space. That’s still a big investment, but if you want sled work without needing turf, plates, or a dedicated training lane, the M1 solves a lot of problems in one very compact package.

What to Consider

Durability and Weather Resistance

Training outdoors comes at the price of not being able to control the elements. Rain or shine, humid or dry, hot or cold—if you’re working out in nature, then you’re subjecting yourself and your gear to Mother Nature’s mood.

We’ll touch on storage below, but look for gear that can withstand moisture, heat, and cold and uses durable materials like water-resistant vinyl and powder-coated steel.

Versatility

Unless you’re planning to build an entire gym outside, you’ll want pieces that work together and let you do more than one exercise. For example, you can anchor a suspension trainer to a pull-up bar and use it to do inverted rows, chest flys, supported squats, and so many more movements.

Adjustable kettlebells and dumbbells offer a range of weights in a single set and let you perform an encyclopedia’s worth of exercises. Think about how you plan to train, and then choose the tool that’ll let you get the most done before adding more to your gym.

Portability and Storage

The best way to increase the lifespan of your equipment is to store it inside, but that means continuously schlepping it back and forth. For this reason, you’ll want to select training tools that, even if they’re not light, can be moved somewhat easily.

A full power rack, for example, isn’t something you can drag in and out of the house. You can, however, shoulder a sandbag, carry adjustable dumbbells from the garage to the driveway, or hang a weight vest on a coat rack when you’re done. Every pick on our list is portable in that sense: useful outdoors, but realistic to store somewhere protected.

Training Surface

Depending on what and how you’re training, your surface matters a lot. Grass is fine for bodyweight work, loaded carries, and lower-impact conditioning, but it can be uneven for heavy lifting. Smooth concrete or asphalt is usually more stable for dumbbell work, kettlebell training, and sled pushes, though rough pavement can be harder on rubber equipment over time.

For repeated slams, throws, or high-impact work, rubber flooring or turf is ideal. For sleds, check the manufacturer’s surface recommendations, since some work better on turf, concrete, or pavement than others. The main rule is simple: Match the tool to the surface, and don’t assume every outdoor space is equally safe for every type of training.

FAQs

What Is the Best Outdoor Fitness Equipment for Beginners?

There isn’t one best piece of gear, but opt for tools you’ll actually train with that are portable and versatile. Suspension trainers, and adjustable kettlebells and dumbbells let you get a lot done for relatively minimal money and clutter.

Can You Leave Fitness Equipment Outside?

You can—especially if you buy outdoor-specific gear with durable coatings—but its better to cover and/or store your gear inside (like within a garage or shed) to extend its lifespan.

What Equipment Do You Need for an Outdoor Workout?

Not much. Your own bodyweight will even do the trick, but start with one strength-training tool, like adjustable dumbbells, and one conditioning-focused tool, like a sled, slam ball, or weighted vest.

What Is the Best Surface for Outdoor Workouts?

It depends, but typically rubber, turf, and smooth concrete are going to be your best bet to drag, push, and slam certain tools on. These surfaces are also more stable than grass, which while easier on your joints, can be more difficult to balance on.
