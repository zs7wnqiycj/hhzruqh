What is Triglycerides?

Triglycerides are the most common type of fat circulating in your bloodstream. They are made up of three fatty acid chains attached to a glycerol backbone, which is why they're called "tri-glycerides." Your body creates them in the liver and intestines from the fats and carbohydrates you eat, especially sugars and starches that aren't immediately needed for energy.

Your body's portable fuel reserve

Triglycerides serve as your primary energy storage molecule. After a meal, excess calories are packaged into triglycerides and stored in fat cells (adipocytes) throughout your body. When you need energy between meals or during physical activity, hormones signal these fat cells to release triglycerides back into the bloodstream.

A window into fat and sugar metabolism

Measuring triglycerides in your blood reveals how efficiently your body processes dietary fats and carbohydrates. The level reflects the balance between how much fat you're consuming or producing and how quickly your cells are using it for fuel. This makes triglycerides a key marker of metabolic health and energy balance.

Why is Triglycerides important?

Triglycerides are the body's primary circulating fat, shuttling energy from meals to cells and storing excess calories in adipose tissue. They reflect how efficiently your metabolism handles dietary fat and carbohydrate, and elevated levels directly accelerate atherosclerosis, increasing risk for heart attack, stroke, and pancreatitis. Normal fasting values sit below 150 mg/dL, with optimal levels closer to 100 or less.

What do my Triglycerides results mean?

Low triglycerides

Low values usually reflect efficient fat clearance or reduced dietary fat intake and absorption. Very low levels may occasionally signal malnutrition, malabsorption syndromes affecting the gut, or rare genetic lipid disorders. In some cases, an overactive thyroid gland speeds metabolism and lowers circulating triglycerides. Low values are generally not a clinical concern unless accompanied by other signs of nutrient deficiency or systemic illness.

Optimal triglycerides

Being in range suggests balanced fat metabolism and effective clearance of dietary and liver-produced lipids from the bloodstream. Most labs define normal as below 150 mg/dL, though optimal cardiovascular health is associated with levels well below that threshold, often under 100 mg/dL. Staying in the lower portion of the reference range reflects stable insulin signaling, healthy liver function, and lower risk for metabolic and vascular complications.

High triglycerides

High values usually reflect excess calorie intake, insulin resistance, or impaired clearance of fat particles from the blood. Elevated triglycerides often accompany metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and fatty liver. They can also rise with excess alcohol, certain medications, untreated hypothyroidism, or kidney disease. Very high levels increase the risk of pancreatitis and contribute to cardiovascular disease through effects on cholesterol particles and inflammation.

Notes on interpretation

Triglycerides fluctuate significantly with recent food intake, so fasting samples are preferred. Pregnancy, acute illness, and some medications can temporarily elevate levels.
