# In Defense of ‘Joker: Folie à Deux’

THE CURRENT ROTTEN Tomatoes scores for Joker: Folie à Deux, the much-awaited sequel to the Academy Award-winning 2019 film Joker, put it firmly in the same range as undeniably crappy superhero flicks Morbius and Madame Web. Folie à Deux's critics score (currently sitting at 33%) is a bit higher than those two (which both sit at 15% or lower), but its audience score (currently at 31%) is significantly lower. What does this tell us? The people who loved the original Joker so much that director/co-writer Todd Phillips and star Joaquin Phoenix returned for a second go-around without that being their original intention absolutely hate this movie (the 2019 film has a 68% from critics and 89% from audiences, for the record).

I'm here to tell you why they're wrong.

Joker: Folie à Deux picks up after the events of the first Joker movie; Arthur Fleck (Phoenix) is a madman imprisoned in Arkham State Hospital after being charged with the murder of five people, and he's awaiting trial. In the hospital, he meets an unhinged woman named Lee (Lady Gaga, playing a version of a Harley Quinn character) who is both infatuated with him on a personal level, and seemingly one of his biggest fans. Not long after the two meet, the movie becomes the two things it clearly wants to be—a musical, and a courtroom drama.

This is part of where Folie à Deux likely began to lose fans of the first film—and, honestly, where it started to win me over. This is probably the right place for me to say that I didn't really care for the original Joker; it wasn't a bad movie, but it's just doing absolutely nothing original. Watch Martin Scorsese's Taxi Driver and The King of Comedy, and you'll see everything Joker was attempting, but done significantly with more depth and tact. As a fan of Phoenix's past performances, I also found it frustrating that he became immortalized with an Oscar for Joker (where he was fine) after doing much better work in movies like Her and The Master. That playing the Joker has become the screen actor's equivalent of playing Hamlet is a whole other conversation.

Just by the sheer nature of becoming a courtroom drama and a musical (at the same time!), Folie à Deux is expressing a level of ambition as a film that the first never did. Add in the fact that Phillips hired a great cinematographer (Lawrence Sher) and a great composer (Hildur Guðnadóttir), and even before we really get into the substance at play, it really looks and sounds like a great movie. If this was called The Trial of the Joker, and the marketing made it more clear that characters were going to be, you know, singing and dancing, audiences may have had far more of an idea on what to expect.

But is it a great movie? Probably not. While some of the musical numbers that come throughout the course of the movie are fun—the first, where Arthur recites a Stevie Wonder song while stomping around a prison rec room, is unexpected and exciting, while later numbers where he's wearing his clown make-up feel like extended and involved stage production pieces—other are just totally dead on arrival. You'll get to a couple songs that feel deliberately low energy and dressed down, and that probably is intentional—but it sure doesn't make for an entertaining movie scene.

Part of what worked for me, here, though, and may not have worked for people who liked (or loved) the original, though, is a disconnect that I always felt with the characters. I've been watching Batman media for my entire life, and one aspect of the Joker that's always been consistent is his standing as a genius. The Joker is an absolute psychopath, someone who could never be trusted and is a danger to himself and everyone around him, but he's also always several steps ahead of everyone around him. That's never felt even a little bit in the first Joker movie; Arthur just feels like a sad, mentally unstable, unintelligent man, having a couple of bad days.

By the end of Folie à Deux, it becomes clear that my thinking wasn't far off from what Phillips was thinking—because there's a strong implication that Arthur really isn't the Joker, after all, and that someone else turns out to be the person who goes toe-to-toe with Batman for all those years.

I understand why fans of the first Joker—and anyone buying into the idea of 'a Joker movie'—would not like the movie that ended up being made here. Since the vast majority of the film takes place either in a mental hospital or a courtroom, there's very little opportunity for "The Joker" to do many "The Joker Things." But, again, I never really thought of this character as a real or accurate depiction of The Joker; instead, it's just a big-budget portrayal of a severely disturbed man who committed several violent crimes, and is dealing with the aftermath. That, combined with some interesting musical numbers, make this at the very least an interesting cinematic endeavor—one that stands out compared to so much of the cookie-cutter stuff that ends up in theaters these days, at the very least.

The performances in the movie are strong, too. Phoenix has far more, internally, to do here than in the 2019 film, and while I'm not a huge fan of the changes they made to Harley Quinn (removing the accent and her origin story as The Joker's psychiatrist), I do think she is perhaps the most interesting person in the entire movie in a vacuum. "Lee" is constantly in control, and Gaga plays her with a great bit of unhinged madness that slowly reveals itself to be string-pulling confidence. Even Industry's Harry Lawtey, who plays a young Harvey Dent (the Gotham City DA who eventually becomes the scarred villain Two-Face), gets a chance to put some slimy, suited-up charm on the same character that Aaron Eckhart and Billy Dee Williams once knocked out of the park.

It also helps that the music is fun! Stevie Wonder, Frank Sinatra? How can you have a bad time when hearing those kinds of tunes? I digress. I understand Joker: Folie à Deux being a polarizing film, because any sequel that is this different from a first installment is naturally going to be fighting an uphill battle. Add in the fact that even by itself, this movie takes swings that big-budget and franchise films don't, and I understand the reception it's gotten. But as more and more people come to Folie à Deux and watch it on its own terms, seeing it as a weird, ambitious, character study about the downfall of a deeply disturbed man, I suspect it will be more and more reclaimed.

If someone wanted a Joker movie, Folie à Deux may not have been what they really wanted. But let's be real: the original Joker—which cribbed Scorsese and focused on a not-particularly-Joker-esque character—wasn't that either.
