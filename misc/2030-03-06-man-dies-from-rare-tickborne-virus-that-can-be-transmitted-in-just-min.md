At the end of April, 74-year-old Charles Smith noticed a tick under his arm. The avid fisherman and hunter didn’t think much of it, since he didn’t develop the “bulls-eye” rash typically seen with tickborne Lyme disease. Initially, his doctor told him there was nothing to worry about. But on May 11, he woke up with a 104-degree fever and chills, WNYT reported.

That’s when he went to the hospital. Doctors tested him for Lyme disease—which came back negative—and treated him for an enlarged kidney, which they thought was to blame. Soon after, he deteriorated, and became paralyzed from the neck down, according to WNYT.

He died on June 6 from brain swelling, TIME reports.

After testing for many possible viruses—including swine flu, since Smith kept pigs in his yard—the family finally got a positive hit for Powassan virus, WYNY said.

Powassan virus is a rare disease spread from infected ticks. It can be transmitted from tick to host within 15 minutes after it attaches, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says. That’s much quicker than with Lyme disease—in most cases, the tick must be attached to the host for about 36 to 48 hours to transmit the bacteria, according to the CDC. (Your gym may be filled with bacteria—find out just how germy it is.)

Many people infected with the virus don’t show symptoms, but if they do, they tend to develop within one week to one month after the bite. Symptoms include fever, headache, vomiting, weakness, loss of coordination, and seizures. The virus can also infect your central nervous system, potentially causing inflammation of your brain or the membranes around your spinal cord, which could be fatal.

About 10 percent of the cases are fatal, and in about half the cases where people recover, they continue to have recurring symptoms like headache or memory problems, the CDC says.

The virus can be serious, but it’s important to note that it’s rare. From 2006 to 2015, an average of seven Powassan viruses were reported each year. But it’s possible it could be growing: States like Minnesota, New Hampshire, and Virginia all reported their first cases within the last seven years.
