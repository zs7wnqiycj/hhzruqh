# Menopause Time Off - Hone Health

A public coalition advancing menopause-inclusive leave and workplace accommodations.

Menopause is the most predictable workforce health event in women's lives, with well-documented symptoms that impact performance and earnings. Yet most workplaces lack programs to support women through it.

MTO exists to close the gap by providing tools and resources for employees and employers alike, and providing grants to help women take time off to recuperate from menopause symptoms.

The MTO Fund is informed by proprietary survey data from 1,028 working women ages 30-60, focused on productivity, focus, participation, and stigma, providing directional evidence of unmet need.

The Menopause Time Off (MTO) Open Declaration

Menopause is a natural life stage experienced by millions of women during their prime working years. Women account for nearly half of the U.S. workforce, and over 40% are age 45 or older. Most women reach menopause between the ages of 45 and 55, and recent estimates show women in this age group account for roughly 20 percent of the entire female workforce in the U.S.

During menopause, most women experience symptoms such as hot flashes, sleep disturbances, cognitive impairments, and mood changes, all of which can significantly disrupt daily life and impact productivity at work.

In a recent Hone Health survey of 1,028 women navigating perimenopause or menopause, nearly 70% report losing up to 10 hours of productivity per week — more than one full day of work — because of symptoms.

A 2025 RAND report estimates the annual economic cost of menopause symptoms at $5.4 billion in lost productivity. Yet data suggests only around 5% of U.S. employers offer menopause-specific benefits.

For many women, menopause shows up at the point they're taking on leadership roles, shaping teams, and mentoring others. When symptoms go untreated, it can quietly drain experience and leadership from organizations. In Hone's survey, one in three women have considered leaving — or have left — the workforce because of menopause symptoms.

Across industries, U.S. workplaces largely lack formal policies, language, or guidance to support employees through menopause. As a result, women must manage the significant physical, cognitive, and emotional changes in silence, often without clear accommodation pathways, protected time off, or stigma-free communication with managers and HR teams.

In Hone's survey, nearly 80% of women said that having clearly defined menopause-related time off or accommodations would improve productivity and reduce stress.

Why This Declaration Exists

Modern workplaces recognize parental leave, mental health support, and flexible work arrangements as essential to long-term productivity and retention. Menopause remains one of the last major health transitions left out of workplace policies.

Ignoring menopause in policy concentrates the costs in individual careers, health, and income. Among the women Hone surveyed, 62% said menopause symptoms affected their earnings, performance review, or career progression in the past year.

This declaration acknowledges that menopause is:

A legitimate workplace issue

A normal transition that intersects with productivity, participation, and wellbeing

A lived experience that affects employees across roles, industries, and socioeconomic backgrounds

What We Affirm

By signing this declaration, we affirm the following principles:

Menopause is a normal life stage that can affect work. Symptoms such as fatigue, brain fog, sleep disruption, hot flashes, and mood changes can impact focus and performance, just as other recognized health transitions do.

Workplace policy has not kept pace with women's lives. The absence of menopause-inclusive benefits ignores the needs of today's workforce.

Women should be able to name menopause without fear. Women should be able to cite menopause as the reason they need time off or flexibility without fear of stigma, judgment, or career consequences.

Menopause-inclusive policy benefits everyone. Organizations that support employees through menopause improve retention, reduce burnout, and protect institutional knowledge, while signaling a culture that takes health and equity seriously.

What We Are Calling For

This declaration does not prescribe a single solution. Instead, it calls for meaningful progress toward menopause-inclusive workplaces through channels which can include:

Offering flexible scheduling and remote options so women can manage symptoms without sacrificing work or sick leave meant for other purposes

Normalizing talking about menopause so that it can be openly cited as a reason for needing time or flexibility

Naming menopause in existing time off or accommodation policies to make that openness official

Connecting employees to resources that can help them navigate this life stage.

A Shared Responsibility

Menopause should not be something employees are expected to manage quietly on their own.

Employers shape the work policies, workloads, and expectations, and with that comes a responsibility to account for major life and health transitions, including menopause. This declaration asks workplaces to treat menopause as part of that duty of care

We recognize that change requires time, experimentation, and dialogue. This declaration is an invitation to employers, policymakers, advocates, and allies to participate in that process.

Our Commitment

Signatories to this declaration commit to:

Acknowledging menopause as a workplace consideration

Engaging in good-faith efforts to improve awareness and support

Treating menopause with the same seriousness afforded to other health conditions and life-stage transitions

This declaration signals a recognition of the impacts of menopause in the workplace and a commitment to build and implement policies and practices that better support employees through it.

About the Founding Organization

Hone combines advanced biomarker testing, clinician-guided care, and hormone and longevity protocols to help women understand what's happening in their bodies and get relief from the symptoms that too often go unmanaged and unacknowledged.
