Gut health is garnering quite a lot of attention these days, and the hype isn’t dying down any time soon. Scientists are finally starting to understand how your microbiome, or the vast variety of bacteria that live within your gut—and entire body—can impact your health. You might assume that bacteria is always harmful, but your body actually depends on some of these microorganisms to function at its best.

Basically, your intestines have more bacterial cells than human cells, and they contain both "good" and "bad" bacteria. These good-for-you bacteria, known as probiotics, live in your gut and help prevent “bad bacteria” from setting up shop, which can impact everything from your immune system to your bowel movements to your skin, explains Jerome Garden, M.D., the director of the Physicians Laser and Dermatology Institute in Chicago.

You can get probiotics from supplements, but you can also take them in naturally through fermented foods like yogurt, olives, sauerkraut, kefir, kimchi, kombucha, and wine, explains Abbey Sharp, R.D., founder of Abbey’s Kitchen. Each of these foods contains their own unique strains of bacteria. Here, six ways these beneficial bugs might do your body good.

PROBIOTICS HELP YOU POOP WHEN YOU'RE CONSTIPATED

The bacteria in your intestines help you digest your food, according to the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health.

There are few things more uncomfortable than not being able to, shall we say, let loose. But probiotics may help you clear out the pipes: After researchers looked at 14 different studies on the effects of probiotics on your bowel movements, they found that taking probiotics can increase your trips to the bathroom, which can ease constipation, according to a meta-analysis published in in The American Journal of Clinical Nutrition.

One reason? Probiotics may help soften the consistency of your stool, allowing it to pass through your bowels more easily, the researchers note.

PROBIOTICS MIGHT BOOST YOUR HEART HEALTH

You always hear about the heart-boosting benefits of the Mediterranean diet, but adding fermented foods to the mix might be good for your ticker, too.

While the effects were modest, preliminary research suggests that probiotics can reduce your blood pressure and LDL (aka “bad”) cholesterol levels, says Sharp. That’s because certain strains of these beneficial bugs may help your body produce more of a blood pressure-regulating enzyme, she explains.

The benefits of a lower BP? A lower risk of stroke, heart attack, and sexual dysfunction, says the American Heart Association.

PROBIOTICS MAY HELP CURE YOUR COLD

Your GI tract is a key player in your immune system, says Sharp, since a large portion of your immune system lies within your gut.

And making sure your gut is full of healthy bacteria can be helpful when cold and flu season hits. In fact, when researchers gave nearly 200 college students a placebo or probiotic supplement for 12 weeks, they found that students caught colds at the same rate, according to a study published in the British Journal of Nutrition. However, those who took probiotics dealt with the symptoms, like a stuffy nose or sore throat, for a shorter period of time.

PROBIOTICS MAY HELP FIGHT DISEASE

Time and time again, scientists find that chronic, body-wide inflammation is a common precursor to many serious health problems, like heart disease and erectile dysfunction. In the short term, inflammation is a good thing, because it helps your body fight off infection, but when it persists, it can then begin to attack your healthy tissue.

And when it comes to diseases in your GI tract, like inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)—a condition in which your body attacks its own tissues—doctors are starting to take note of probiotics as a treatment, says Anton Bilchik, M.D., Ph.D., professor of surgery and chief of gastrointestinal research at John Wayne Cancer Institute at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica.

Chronic inflammation can also damage your DNA, which may set the stage for cancer-causing mutations over time. Several animal studies have found, however, that probiotics may help slow or even prevent the progression of various cancers, particularly colon cancer, due to their anti-inflammatory properties, says Dr. Bilchik.
