# Danny Boyle Called Me From Church to Talk About '28 Years Later'

[Church to Talk](https://tissue-renewal.china-akan.com/)

The following story contains some spoilers for 28 Years Later.

DANNY BOYLE IS Zoom calling me from church—and he's excited about it. Mostly because this isn't just any church, and he's not there just for fun or spiritual clarity. This church is one of six that was designed by the famed English architect Nicholas Hawksmoor in the 1700s, but, more importantly for the purposes of our conversation, it's the location where he shot an iconic sequence for 2003's 28 Days Later, the horror hit that's endured for decades. You know the scene. And while the 68-year-old filmmaker is in a great mood, wearing a black blazer over a t-shirt with a big smile on his face, he's clearly got horror on the mind.

"Very interesting to read about these churches that were built in the 18th century and there's all sorts of rumors that they're linked to satanic forces and..." he says before trailing off. "Anyway, they're amazing churches, which survived the blitz, and we're doing a screening tonight."

The movie he's screening at the church isn't 28 Days Later, but rather that movie's follow up, 28 Years Later, which hit theaters last week and is already a smash with critics and fans alike. While a sequel that he didn’t direct, 28 Weeks Later, was released in 2007, Years finds Boyle back at the helm, collaborating once again with Alex Garland (who wrote the first film, and also collaborated with Boyle on 2007's Sunshine; Garland's novel, The Beach, was also the source material for Boyle's 2000 film of the same name).

28 Years Later features a number of remarkable performances from its cast, including Jodie Comer, Aaron Taylor-Johnson, Ralph Fiennes, and Alfie Williams, who plays the film's tween protagonist. It's got innovative cinematography, with kill scenes that will hype up even the most experienced horror buff. And it's got intense tone shifts, going from terrifying in one moment to tear-jerking in the next. It may not be what you expect in a 28 Years Later movie, but it's executed to perfection, and totally pays off.

And it’s also without question the kind of movie you see and just want to talk to someone about. So, I figured, why not talk to the director himself? This is why the Academy Award-winning director was Zooming me from the depths of a famous, possibly satanic church on a Wednesday in June.

MEN'S HEALTH: What kind of reaction and feedback have you been getting now that 28 Years Later has been out for a little bit?

DANNY BOYLE: The big thing—and Sony was very concerned about this, obviously, and they tried to disguise it—is that people are shocked at how emotional it is. Because the climax of 28 Days Later was so violent, albeit romantic, people forget that it was actually moving in its own way, with Brendan Gleeson trying to save his daughter and all that kind of stuff.

Sony was worried that the horror fans would be put off, but the reactions have been lovely. People have been talking about how they were surprised that they ended up crying. It’s been really nice that we can finally be open about it! That's why they kept screening only the first 28 minutes to everyone. I was going, "Why won't you show them the whole film?"

MH: Did you and Alex always intend for the movie to have such drastic tone shifts?

DB: Yes. It’s a very unusual structure, really, because the journey repeats itself, with the boy. It's the same boy, but with a different parent. In the first part they're avoiding Kelson [Ralph Fiennes's character], albeit it isn't a big deal yet, but he’s someone they could have invited back to that island. They don't have a doctor, clearly, because she's not being treated. But they didn't invite him because they think he's insane.

That movement towards this inverted Kurtz [Marlon Brando's character in Apocalypse Now] figure is an unusual one, but we were aware that it was going to shift. There was still action and jeopardy and threats and all those kind of things. In fact, more intense, in a way, because you don't have Aaron there, who appears to be able to handle anything—or thinks he can. What was really interesting, and it's a funny thing to say because you're directing it, but you spot things in editing that you haven't really noticed when you're shooting. It's really weird.

You think, "Oh." And people say "Don't admit that." But it's true: Alfie's performance as Spike changes because of the influence he's under. With Aaron, it's very physical, it's very Gung ho, and he's kicking and screaming and doing all that. But with the mother, it's a much more internalized performance. He still does the action stuff when those sequences happen, but it's much more internalized and he's moving towards a real maturity, which he achieves by the end. He decides not to follow in his father's footsteps, but to make his own way… or to a very dangerous place, as it turns out. But we'll see more of that in the second film.

MH: Speaking of Aaron in 28 Years Later, it’s fascinating that your films often follow these flawed male characters. Ewan McGregor in Trainspotting and Michael Fassbender in Steve Jobs are just a couple other examples. What is it about that type of person that you like bringing to the screen?

DB: I think we're obsessed with them. Myself and the writers I work with, clearly, we're obsessed with them. Our natural instinct is that if we’re going to base a film around a hero, especially a male hero, then he's going to be flawed. He's not going to be pure, and he's going to have serious deficiencies that are going to be either entertaining or revealing—and hopefully both.

MH: One of the movie’s major new creations is the ‘Alpha,’ which is a bigger and stronger infected entity than anything we’ve seen before. What goes into creating something like that?

DB: The first stage is that you meet the actors, because I'm always very actor driven. I couldn't do those movies where they create a suit and then hire an actor who'll fit into it. In fact, a couple of the actors who play the Alphas have done roles like that, where they've been brought in just to wear a suit and then the behavior is worked out based on the suit.

I do it the other way around. Even though you’re still preparing elements and prosthetics that they're going to wear, I like to work with the actors. I had an incredible rehearsal with Chi Lewis-Parry, who plays Samson, the main Alpha, and I remember it vividly. He was at the other end of the room and he had to attack me and not hurt me, but appear to, and it was pretty terrifying.

If you've spoken to him, he's the sweetest, gentlest man. Those big guys are very sweet. They don't have to try very hard, because everybody just gives them what they want anyway. But when he turns it on, boy he can act. He's like one of your guys. You know those actors? There's half a dozen of them now, who've come from wrestling or mixed martial arts, and often become very good actors.

Wait until you see him in the second film, where he's excellent. His character gets developed a lot.

MH: The film also employs these 360 degree shots, done with a very cool iPhone rig, a few times when some of the infected are being killed via bow and arrow. They are so cool—how did you come up with that idea?

DB: Its root is The Matrix, obviously. It's bullet time in The Matrix, and I'm astonished that's not been used more since. The Matrix is 1999. It's like, "Come on guys!" It's a beautiful way of flinging the audience into 3D, and then out again. Because you’re watching a two-dimensional experience, where everybody talks about immersive now— You're watching a flat screen. Until that changes, you're watching a flat screen. And so, one of the things you do as a director is try and create, within those two dimensions, as much that feels as visceral as possible, and this to me is the ultimate. Unless you put special glasses on, and nobody does that anymore.

But just for a moment, you get flung inside and then flipped back out again. You see around the back and around the front again, in the same motion, and I love that. I think in 50 years time, we'll be doing that in films. We'll be going inside them or they'll be going inside us. I don't quite know whether it'll work. It won't be in my lifetime, but it'll be some process of that, because it's a natural part of that experience of the visceral, which is what I love about these kind of movies.

MH: It really does feel like you’re getting pulled into the movie. That’s a perfect description.

DB: Yeah, but it's flat and you defy that. And the greatest defying of that was obviously The Matrix, which was just mind-blowing. But this is a poor man's bullet time, because it's maneuverable. It's movable. You can take it places, you can walk with it, you can shove it into corners of a room—I would use it again for the right film. Who knows what the third 28 Years Later film will be like? I would definitely use it again. I've learned a lot about how to use it.

MH: I love Jack O’Connell so much at the end as the Jimmy character. Did you have any idea he was going to be showing up in this not long after he also had such a great role in Sinners, another horror-adjacent film?

DB: I knew he was going off to do the Ryan Coogler film, but I didn't know what it was about and he didn't mention what it was about, and it's so original. I was so excited to see him in it, and he's so different. You could think one of your reactions might well be, "Oh god, no!" But he's so original in everything that he does. When he played in Back to Black, the Amy Winehouse movie, that's a very difficult part and he brings such an intrigue and humanity. He brings both to people, and he does it with this guy, Sir Lord Jimmy Crystal.

I've seen a rough cut of the second movie, and he and Ralph Fiennes are extraordinary in it. I mean, properly. It's not like the first film—it isn't a huge journey film, it's a proper performance film and he's incredible in it.

MH: You mentioned the second film, and I know that’s already been shot and directed by Nia DaCosta. If you get the chance to make the third one, can you tease anything from it, and do you have any idea when you’d get started?

DB: It really depends on Sony now, because they're obviously in first position to give us a budget for it, and then Alex will write it. There's a fully fleshed scenario outlined. And obviously it's a big part for Cillian [Murphy], whose role in the second film is a bit like Jack O'Connell's in the first film. He appears in this captivating ending, beautifully introduced by Nia. It's beautifully set up, and again, you just go, "Oh, God. I really want to see."

These are summer films. They have to be shot in the summer, because they're mostly outdoors, there's no electricity, all that kind of stuff. There are some night scenes, there's a great night sequence in the second film, right at the end, but most of the both films are daytime. And to do that in Britain, you have to shoot in the summer. In theory, it would shoot next summer.

MH: 28 Years Later also marks a reunion between yourself and Alex Garland. You first worked together adapting his book, The Beach, back in 2000—that means 25 years of collaboration. Are you guys friends? Do you hang out?

DB: I'd say we are friends. But we don't hang out. I don't know whether you have people at work who are like that—you're really quite close, except you don't hang out.

MH: Of course.

DB: It doesn't develop a hangout relationship, and it's funny. We get on really well, and I think it was really interesting working with him again, because he’s been off doing his own directing, and that's taught him a lot. The load you have to carry that people don't see until you try it. So, if anything, that deepened the respect.

We did a lot of work together on the script, and he always gives brilliant notes. Lots of people give notes, but most are not worth listening to. But when you get a good note, Evan, it's worth a great deal. Even if it's tough, even if you don't want to hear it, there's another part of you thinks, "Oh, fuck. That's a really good note." [Laughs] I should change that and I don't really want to, but I should and I will. It's really good and you need that respect in order to do that.

MH: Is there another movie—not one of your own—that you’d want to see a 20+ years later sequel to?

DB: It is funny, isn't it? Because the genre ones have usually had a sequel. But Near Dark, directed by Kathryn Bigelow.

I love that movie. She is so underrated as a director. Near Dark would definitely be one. Because every other one you think of, there’s been a sequel, like Blade Runner. I was trying to think of a Ridley [Scott], but Ridley has either sanctioned or made a sequel to the big ones, and you can't make one to Thelma and Louise. So, I don't think you could do that. But Near Dark would be a good one.

MH: On a similar note, part of what I admire about your career is that you're never boxed in by one specific genre. Even as we’ve discussed with 28 Years Later, there’s a lot at play. Is there a genre you haven't done that you would want to try?

DB: An original musical. It’s the toughest, toughest, toughest thing to do. An original musical with original music and an original story. We made a film 20 years ago called Millions, a kids' film. And me and the writer, Frank Cottrell-Boyce, wondered if we should do it with music. And if I could turn back time, I would. We were going to ask Noel Gallagher from Oasis to write the songs, because you can only do it in certain stories. There's certain scenarios where people are allowed to sing and you accept it as an audience. And there's all those where it's just laughable and you just won't—you'll never get it off the ground anyway.

But that was one where these boys, because it was a story of two brothers, they could have sung and it would've broke your heart. And it was set in and around Manchester and Liverpool, so Noel Gallagher would've been great. If we were doing it now, I'd do it in a heartbeat.

I saw Emilia Pérez last year and that was... I loved that. He's a wonderful director and he did it with that, where people just start singing, and you just think "Oh, right. Okay." And they're dancing, and it's like... I love that. I don't know whether I've got enough time left to do one, but I'd love to do one of those. Honestly!

DB: Where are you going to go and see them?

MH: I'm going to the first Manchester show.

MH: Oh, gosh. I've always been a fan of “Some Might Say.” I think “Slide Away” is a recent favorite. “Acquiesce”? I'm going to call those my top three.

DB: I don't know what they think about “The Hindu Times.” I love “The Hindu Times.” That guitar in “The Hindu Times” just does it for me. I haven't got tickets to see them, but maybe I will go. Yeah, maybe I'll go and see them.
