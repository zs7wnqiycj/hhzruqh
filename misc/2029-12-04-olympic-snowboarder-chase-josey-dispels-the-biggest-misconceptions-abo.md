The athletes of the 2026 Milano-Cortina Winter Games will give their all to go for gold on the snow and ice—but first, they'll have a chance to share everything you want to know about their sports while they're on the road. This is the Men's Health Muscle Car.

SNOWBOARDER CHASE JOSEY joined fitness director Ebenezer Samuel for a quick ride in the MH Muscle Car to talk about his Olympic workouts and how he prepped for the halfpipe snowboarding competition.

The 30-year-old from Idaho is a three-time Team USA member, having competed in the snowboard halfpipe at the 2018 PyeongChang and 2022 Beijing Games. He recently competed in his third consecutive Olympic halfpipe final at Milano-Cortina. He earned a score of 70.25, landing him in 11th place.

If you didn't get a chance to watch Josey in action, he provided a brief rundown of what to know about halfpipe snowboarding. Fundamentally, Josey said it's a blend of style and technical tricks. "We drop in and get like five hits in the pipe," he explained. "We try to go as big as we can, spin and land gracefully, and put a little style on it."

Watch or read on below to learn how Josey trains for halfpipe snowboarding, what non-Olympic sport he'd win a gold medal in, and his honest thoughts on Italian food.

MEN'S HEALTH: When I think of different sports, basketball is about explosiveness. Weightlifting is about pure strength. What do you see as the biggest physical challenge for your sport?

CHASE JOSEY: A misconception of halfpipe is that everyone thinks the tricks are the hardest part. But when you train enough, the tricks become the easy part. When you're in the air, the real difficult part happens within the halfpipe. When you're in contact with the snow and riding the edge all the way through the lip. Once you pop up in the air, it's like you're weightless and floating. It feels nice. Then you land and you try to get all your speed back for that next hit.

MH: What muscle groups do you really have to train and hit hard?

CJ: For us, it's basically the whole posterior chain. Legs, obviously, but also calves, hammies, and glutes because you're bent over so it's a lot of strength to bend your knees, pull your chest up, and stay standing.

MH: What's your go-to exercise? Or is there one exercise you always make sure to get in the workout?

CJ: Lot of mobility, a good warm up, and then single leg squats are good. Just the traditional squat, kind of quick, reactive stuff. Just being really explosive and fast with your movements. We're not necessarily the biggest guys—I'm like 155 [pounds] on a good day.

MH: I've heard from a couple of winter athletes that they don't train hard on upper body. Do you train upper body at all?

CJ: I do. I just like it. It might not be as important for snowboarding, but it's important for the general head-to-toe physique. A lot of pullups, pushups. I'm a big fan of yoga. You got to blend it all because if you're in the gym, 365 days, I don't know if that's going to really pay dividends.

MH: Let's shift gears and talk about the Olympic experience. How has it been in terms of finding good protein?

CJ: Well, the food in Italy has been awesome. Lot of really good pizza and pasta. I'm kind of a Carbonara fanatic. So this is a good place to get that.

MH: When you're out in the cold, how do you get yourself warmed up?

CJ: Going back to the warm up, it's just a dynamic kind of flexion and mobility. Get the heart rate up just a little bit. Once you're riding it can be pretty cold out but you're going to stay warm. Keep moving is the secret.

MH: What's it been like in the Olympic Village, hanging out with the other athletes? Are you guys spending a lot of time together? Do you just stay with your team?

CJ: Yeah, most of my time is spent with my roommates. There's some of my really close friends, Chase Blackwell and Jake Bates.

MH: Let's hit the accelerator on this interview. Strength or cardio, which do you train more?

CJ: Strength.

MH: Protein or fiber, which do you care about more?

CJ: Protein.
