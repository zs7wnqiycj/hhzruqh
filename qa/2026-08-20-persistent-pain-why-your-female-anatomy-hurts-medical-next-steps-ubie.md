Persistent Pain? Why Your Female Anatomy Hurts & Medical Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Persistent pelvic, abdominal, vulvar, or breast pain can stem from menstrual issues, endometriosis, ovarian cysts or fibroids, infections like PID or UTIs, pelvic floor dysfunction, pregnancy ligament strain, or GI conditions; pain that lasts weeks or disrupts daily life should be assessed.

Next steps include tracking symptoms and seeing a clinician for a pelvic exam, labs, STI testing, and imaging, with treatments ranging from NSAIDs and hormonal therapy to pelvic floor physical therapy or surgery. Seek urgent care now for sudden severe pain, heavy bleeding, fever, fainting, or pregnancy red flags. There are several factors to consider that could change your plan, see the complete guidance below.

Explanation

Persistent pain in the female anatomy can be confusing, frustrating, and sometimes frightening. You may wonder whether what you're feeling is "normal," hormonal, muscular, or something more serious. The truth is that pain in the female body—especially in the pelvis, abdomen, breasts, or reproductive organs—has many possible causes. Some are harmless and temporary. Others require medical treatment.

Understanding what may be happening in your body is the first step toward relief.

Because these structures are closely connected, pain can overlap or feel difficult to pinpoint. Hormones also play a major role in how pain is experienced throughout the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause.

Persistent pain is generally defined as discomfort lasting more than a few weeks or pain that keeps coming back.

Common Causes of Persistent Pain in Female Anatomy

1. Menstrual-Related Pain (Dysmenorrhea)

Cramping during your period is common. However, severe or worsening cramps are not something you should ignore.

Pain may be caused by:

Uterine contractions

Hormone-like chemicals called prostaglandins

Underlying conditions such as endometriosis or fibroids

If over-the-counter pain medication doesn't help or your pain disrupts daily life, it's time to speak to a doctor.

2. Endometriosis

Endometriosis occurs when tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus. It can cause:

Severe menstrual cramps

Pain during sex

Chronic pelvic pain

Infertility

Pain with bowel movements

This condition is common but often underdiagnosed. Persistent pelvic pain that worsens around your period should be evaluated by a healthcare provider.

3. Ovarian Cysts

Ovarian cysts are fluid-filled sacs on the ovary. Many are harmless and resolve on their own. However, larger or ruptured cysts can cause:

Sudden sharp pain

Pelvic pressure

Bloating

Nausea

Severe sudden pain, especially with fever or dizziness, requires urgent medical care.

4. Uterine Fibroids

Fibroids are non-cancerous growths in the uterus. They can lead to:

Heavy menstrual bleeding

Lower back pain

Frequent urination

Some fibroids cause no symptoms, but persistent pain or heavy bleeding should be discussed with a doctor.

5. Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

PID is an infection of the reproductive organs. It often results from untreated sexually transmitted infections.

Symptoms may include:

Lower abdominal pain

Fever

Unusual discharge

Irregular bleeding

PID needs prompt medical treatment to prevent long-term complications such as infertility.

6. Urinary Tract Issues

The bladder sits close to reproductive organs in female anatomy. Conditions like urinary tract infections (UTIs) or interstitial cystitis can cause:

Burning with urination

Pelvic pain

Frequent urge to urinate

Pressure in the lower abdomen

Persistent bladder pain should not be ignored.

7. Round Ligament Pain (Common in Pregnancy)

During pregnancy, the ligaments supporting the uterus stretch. This can cause sharp or pulling pain in the lower abdomen or groin.

The pelvic floor muscles support the uterus, bladder, and rectum. When these muscles are too tight or too weak, you may experience:

Pelvic floor physical therapy can often provide significant relief.

9. Gastrointestinal Causes

Not all pelvic pain originates from reproductive organs. Digestive issues such as:

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)

Constipation

Inflammatory bowel disease

can mimic pain related to female anatomy. Your doctor may evaluate multiple systems to determine the true source.

When Pain Is a Medical Emergency

While many causes of pain are manageable, some symptoms require urgent attention. Seek immediate care if you experience:

Sudden, severe abdominal or pelvic pain

Fainting or dizziness

Fever over 101°F (38.3°C)

Heavy vaginal bleeding (soaking a pad in under an hour)

Severe pain during pregnancy

These symptoms can signal serious conditions that need emergency care.

Why You Shouldn't Ignore Persistent Pain

Many women normalize pain because they've been told "bad cramps are normal" or "it's just hormones." While some discomfort is common, chronic or worsening pain is not something you have to live with.

Persistent pain can affect:

Mental health

Sleep

Relationships

Fertility

What to Expect at a Doctor's Appointment

If you decide to seek care, your provider may:

Ask detailed questions about your symptoms

Review your menstrual history

Perform a pelvic exam

Order blood tests

Recommend ultrasound imaging

Consider STI testing

Suggest MRI or laparoscopy in complex cases

Be honest about your symptoms, even if they feel embarrassing. Your healthcare provider's goal is to help you feel better.

Treatment Options for Pain in Female Anatomy

Treatment depends on the cause but may include:

Medications

Anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)

Hormonal birth control

Antibiotics (for infection)

Pain management medications

Hormonal Therapy

Birth control pills

Hormonal IUD

GnRH medications (for endometriosis)

Physical Therapy

Pelvic floor therapy

Stretching and strengthening exercises

Lifestyle Adjustments

Heat therapy

Regular movement

Stress reduction

Dietary changes

Surgical Options

Removal of cysts or fibroids

Laparoscopy for endometriosis

In rare cases, hysterectomy

Your treatment plan should be personalized to your body, symptoms, and goals.

How to Track Your Symptoms

Keeping a symptom journal can help your doctor identify patterns. Track:

Timing of pain

Severity (1–10 scale)

Menstrual cycle phase

Activities that trigger pain

Associated symptoms (fever, discharge, nausea)

This information can make diagnosis faster and more accurate.

Taking Control of Your Health

Persistent pain in the female anatomy is common, but it is not something you should simply accept. Your body is signaling that something needs attention. In many cases, the cause is treatable, manageable, or temporary.

The key steps are:

Don't ignore ongoing pain

Use reliable symptom check tools when appropriate

Seek medical evaluation when symptoms persist

Speak openly with your doctor

Advocate for yourself if pain continues

Most importantly, if you experience severe, sudden, or potentially life-threatening symptoms, speak to a doctor or seek emergency medical care immediately.

You deserve answers. You deserve relief. And you deserve to understand what your body is telling you.
