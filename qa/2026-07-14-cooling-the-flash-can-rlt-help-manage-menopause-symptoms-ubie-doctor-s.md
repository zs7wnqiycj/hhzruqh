Explanation

Menopause brings a range of changes—hot flashes, night sweats, mood swings and more—as your body adjusts to new hormone levels. Many women search for gentle, non-drug options to ease these symptoms. One treatment gaining attention is red light therapy (RLT). In this article, we'll explore what red light therapy for menopause and night sweats is, how it might work, what the research says, and practical tips if you decide to give it a try.

What Is Red Light Therapy?

Red light therapy uses low-level wavelengths of red or near-infrared light to penetrate the skin. It's sometimes called photobiomodulation or low-level laser (or light) therapy. Unlike tanning beds or UV treatments, RLT does not damage DNA or increase skin cancer risk.

How Might RLT Help with Menopause Symptoms?

While most RLT research focuses on pain and skin conditions, some theories suggest it could ease menopause complaints:

Improved Circulation

Red light can stimulate nitric oxide release in blood vessels, potentially improving blood flow.

Better circulation may help regulate body temperature and reduce the intensity of hot flashes.

Hormonal Balance Support

Preliminary lab studies indicate that red and near-infrared light might influence cellular activity in hormone-producing tissues.

Though direct effects on estrogen levels haven't been conclusively proven, some users report fewer mood swings.

Reduced Inflammation

RLT has anti-inflammatory properties.

Chronic low-grade inflammation can worsen joint aches and fatigue during menopause.

Enhanced Sleep Quality

Night sweats often disrupt sleep cycles.

Red light may support melatonin production (the sleep hormone), helping you drift off and stay asleep.

Stress and Mood Regulation

By promoting mitochondrial activity, RLT can boost energy at the cellular level.

A small pilot study found that light therapy reduced anxiety in women undergoing menopause transition.

What Does the Research Say?

Current data on red light therapy for menopause and night sweats is limited but promising:

A 2019 pilot study involving 30 women showed that daily near-infrared light to the sternum for two weeks reduced the frequency of hot flashes by about 30%.

Another small trial reported improved sleep quality and decreased joint pain after four weeks of full-body red light exposure.

Limitations to keep in mind:

Most studies are small and lack control groups.

Protocols vary widely—different wavelengths, durations and device types.

Long-term safety and effectiveness specifically for menopause haven't been established.

When to Seek Professional Help

While red light therapy is low risk, severe menopause symptoms can sometimes mask or coincide with other health issues. You should speak to a doctor if you experience:

Very heavy or irregular bleeding

Chest pain, shortness of breath or dizziness

Severe depression or thoughts of self-harm
