# Super Shoes Have Changed Distance Running. Do You Actually Need Them for Your Best Race?

THE MORNING OF the Berlin Marathon, I stood packed into my corral with a few thousand other first-timers waiting for the countdown to enter the course. Around me, everyone seemed to be wearing super shoes, the carbon-plated, foam-loaded models many runners now swear by for racing. I looked down at my own feet and felt a bit out of place. I had laced up the Adidas Adizero Evo SL, a model without a carbon plate.

I picked my footwear because I had trained in the shoes for weeks and knew they would be comfortable for long miles. I wasn't going to set any course records with my leisurely 12-minute-mile pace, and finishing without punishing my body in the 80-degree heat was my main goal. My race day pick was a practical decision.

But since super shoes came on the road running scene in 2016, elite athletes have gotten a lot faster. Since then, world records have been smashed by runners in super shoes from multiple major brands. World Athletics—the governing body for running and track and field—now requires that any footwear used in competition be commercially available to the public and have a midsole no thicker than 40 millimeters. Still, the idea is potent: Special shoes result in snappier strides, which equals faster race times.

Watching these athletes made it easy to understand why so many recreational runners reach for the same gear: If a pair of shoes can shave minutes off the pros’ times, maybe they can make the rest of us just a little faster, too. Standing in the sea of brightly-colored, high stack shoes, I couldn't help but wonder: Would all these other runners actually get what they were hoping for out of these shoes?

What Runners Need to Know About Super Shoes

Super shoes were originally designed for elite athletes chasing record-setting marathon times. The combination of a stiff carbon plate, rocker shape, and high-rebound foam creates a type of “cantilever effect” that propels you forward, says Dr. Ben Pearl a fellow at the American Academy of Podiatric Sports Medicine. For runners moving at a 7-minute mile and faster (pacing around 3 hours for the full marathon distance), studies show that certain super shoes can improve running economy (essentially, the amount of energy used to run) between three and four percent compared to models without the high tech features. “There’s real benefit for folks who are trying to optimize efficiency and set a PR when they’re high-level runners,” says Colleen Brough, DPT, clinical specialist in orthopedic physical therapy and director of the Columbia RunLab.

That potential performance bump has led many recreational runners to believe to run their best, they need a super shoe for race day. “When it comes to super shoes, the runners who come see me are kind of obsessed,” says Brough. According to Strava’s 2024 Year in Sport Report, 44 percent of marathoners wore super shoes, up 14 percent from 2023. But according to Brough, the kicks are not as effective for the average runner as they are for elite athletes. “In my experience, it would be hard for you to convince me that a super shoe is going to give a substantial benefit to a newbie runner, or a runner who's at your 10-minute-mile pace or slower,” she adds. Once you drop to a typical mid-pack marathon pace (about 3.5 hours, or 8 minutes per mile), some studies suggest the benefit shrinks to maybe one percent.

That’s because most beginner and intermediate runners haven’t optimized their running form. Brough explains that there are three parts to your gait: the initial contact, when your heel, midfoot, or forefoot first touches the ground; the mid-stance, when the leg is directly underneath your body; and the push-off phase, or terminal stance—when the leg extends behind you. “That’s where you get a bit of spring when you’re using super shoes,” says Brough. “In order to get that benefit, you need to be able to reach terminal stance.” The vast majority of recreational runners barely get out of mid-stance, she says.

There are also potential issues in how this type of footwear might affect runners' bodies. While one preliminary study suggests that super shoes may reduce the load on the ankle joint and calves by encouraging a mid- or fore-foot strike, others point to a higher risk of pain and bone-stress injuries because of the stiff plate and the way it alters mechanics. Overall, there’s not enough evidence one way or another to say carbon-plated shoes prevent or cause injuries.

But the health experts recommend that runners consider their specific needs before choosing to wear the specialized sneakers. Pearl says that runners with excessive ankle pronation or heavier body weight may want to steer clear of super shoes, since they’re less stable than traditional models and the lightweight materials tend to wear down much quicker than those used in traditional shoes. “A runner who compresses the midsole more will go through that critical foam much faster," says Pearl. "Once the midsole bottoms out or wears down asymmetrically, the runner is going to end up being more susceptible to injuries like shin splints, plantar fasciitis, or runner's knee.”

According to the experts, speed shouldn't be the only factor in your footwear decisions, anyway—even if you are dead-set on running as fast as you possibly can. Comfort is also an important element of performance. “There’s powerful data demonstrating that the best shoes for us are the ones that genuinely feel good,” says Brough. Some runners—regardless of pace—will really love the feel of a certain super shoe model, while others will be more comfortable with the less dramatic experience that comes with a "normal" running shoe.

Should You Wear Super Shoes?

If you love your super shoes, you can wear them, no matter your pace. Even a one-percent boost in running economy can add up, and if lacing them up makes running more fun, that’s reason enough to keep at it. One small study even suggests that wearing super shoes can make you feel like running is easier and more comfortable. I love to wear the New Balance SuperComp Elite 4 for anything less than half-marathon distance, and I’ve tested other super shoes like the On Cloudboom Strike and felt like I was absolutely flying (even if my Strava recap says differently).

If you do choose to wear super shoes, the experts advise to add them into your routine slowly. The stiff plate may aggravate underlying issues, and the rocker shape could change your running gait. “When you introduce a new motion path, you could be throwing a monkey wrench in that system if it's introduced too quickly and too frequently,” says Pearl. Start training in them for short runs or speedwork, and then build up to longer distances before your race.

Fortunately, there are many options that mimic the energy return and propulsion of a racing shoe with bouncy foams and rocker geometry, just without the carbon plate (see below for some of our top picks). I chose to wear the shoe I did in Berlin because, well, I am not fast, and after five hours of pounding pavement, a stiff racing shoe sounded like the opposite of what I needed. The sneakers felt responsive but forgiving, and my shins weren’t demolished by 26.2 miles. I've had similar experiences running in models from other brands, too.

There's also the matter of cost. Running has long been held up as an accessible sport for the masses—all you need is a pair of sneakers and the road, the saying goes—but the high bill for super shoes can make it feel like your next PR has a price tag. Add to that social pressure; super shoes have now become a type of status symbol or a sign to others that you take your training seriously. “Think about how expensive the races have become as well,” says Pearl. “Now you have a selective group, where there’s this upper echelon of both fitness and economics.” Maybe that’s why so many runners buy into the hype.

But you don’t need a $300 pair of shoes to prove that you belong on the course. The medal around your neck at the end of your race does that just fine. The most important thing is that you wear what you want, especially if your kicks incentivize you to go after your goals and have fun. Just be sure to toe the line with clear eyes about what the material benefits might actually be for you.

I was happy to finish my first marathon feeling proud that my body carried me through 26.2 miles—and I was especially thrilled that I could walk without pain the next day and get back to training quickly. For me, that was worth far more than blending in with the crowd.
