Can You Get Social Security Disability for Severe Nerve Pain?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Severe nerve pain that prevents you from working for at least 12 months may qualify you for Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI). Eligibility is determined under the SSA's Blue Book Section 11.14 (Peripheral Neuropathy) or through a Residual Functional Capacity (RFC) assessment showing your condition limits your ability to perform work-related tasks.

To qualify, you must meet specific medical listings, submit objective diagnostic test results, provide detailed treatment records, and successfully navigate the application or appeals process. Key criteria include documented disorganization of motor function in two extremities or marked limitations in physical and mental functioning.

Because nerve pain has many underlying causes—each with different treatments and disability implications—understanding your specific condition is the critical first step. Explanation

If you're struggling with severe nerve pain (neuropathy) that prevents you from working, you may wonder if you qualify for Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI). Applying for "Disability for neuropathy" can feel overwhelming, but understanding the criteria, gathering the right evidence, and following a clear process can improve your chances of approval.

Understanding Neuropathy and Its Impact

Neuropathy refers to damage or dysfunction of peripheral nerves. Common causes include diabetes, chemotherapy, autoimmune diseases, infections, and traumatic injuries. Symptoms often include:

Sharp, shooting pains

Numbness or muscle weakness

Balance problems or difficulty walking

When nerve pain is severe, even simple daily tasks—like buttoning a shirt or walking across a room—can become impossible. If your symptoms have lasted (or are expected to last) at least 12 months and severely limit your ability to work, you may qualify for SSDI.

Social Security's Medical Listing for Peripheral Neuropathies

The Social Security Administration (SSA) publishes the "Blue Book" of impairments. Section 11.14 specifically addresses peripheral neuropathies:

11.14A (Motor and Sensory Disturbance)You must show both:

Motor loss: extreme muscle weakness in one or more limbs; OR

Sensory loss: marked loss of touch or pain sensation; PLUS

Extreme limitation in performing fine and gross movements, standing, or walking.

11.14B (Disorganization of Motor Function)Demonstrate significant interference in movement or coordination (e.g., ataxia, reduced muscle control).

Meeting these listings automatically qualifies you for benefits ("medical listing level"). If you don't meet the exact criteria, you can still qualify through a Residual Functional Capacity (RFC) assessment.

Maximizing Your Chances of Approval

Document Everything: Keep thorough records of every doctor visit, test, and treatment.

Use Clear Language: Ask your provider to describe how nerve pain limits your daily activities and work.

Functional Reports: Fill out SSA's "Activities of Daily Living" forms carefully.

Stay Consistent: Report pain levels and limitations consistently in all forms and interviews.

Consider Legal Help: An attorney or accredited representative can guide you through appeals, if necessary.

Residual Functional Capacity (RFC)

If you don't meet a medical listing, SSA assesses your RFC—what you can still do despite neuropathy. Factors include:

Sitting, standing, walking durations

Lifting or carrying limits

Ability to handle small objects (buttons, tools)

Environmental restrictions (e.g., vibration, temperature extremes)

Your RFC is matched against job requirements. If no jobs exist that you can perform, you may still win SSDI benefits.

Other Benefits and Resources

Supplemental Security Income (SSI): For people with limited income and resources.

State Disability Programs: Some states offer short-term or long-term disability.

Vocational Rehabilitation: Helps you find suitable work if you can perform some jobs.

Final Thoughts

Securing Social Security Disability for severe nerve pain is challenging but possible. The key is thorough documentation, clear evidence of functional limitations, and understanding the Blue Book listings for peripheral neuropathies. Don't be discouraged by initial denials—many applicants succeed on appeal. Above all, work closely with your healthcare team, maintain honest records of your pain and limitations, and seek professional guidance when needed. Good luck on your journey to obtaining the support you deserve.

Remember: always speak to a doctor about any new or serious health concerns.
