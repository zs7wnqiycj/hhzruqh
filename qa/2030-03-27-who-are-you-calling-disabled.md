# Who Are You Calling Disabled?

He Did This: Became a triathlon gold medalist

In Spite Of: Being blind

When 24-year-old Aaron Scheidies was a kid, he heard about the Ironman Triathlon World Championships, in Kona, Hawaii. Scheidies decided it was his destiny. Never mind that he lived in Michigan. Or that in second grade he came down with Stargardt's disease, a form of macular degeneration that leads to blindness in children and young adults. Even as his vision was failing, Scheidies began to enter triathlons, pushing toward his dream. "Competing in triathlons kept me motivated and energetic and positive," he says. He raced without a guide, tracing the course before-hand with his parents, identifying landmarks and potholes, and then using his near-perfect memory to guide him through the bike and run legs of the race. He followed the sound of other swimmers' splashes to stay on course. Soon, he started winning races, and last summer, he competed in his first Ironman, finishing the Coeur D'Alene in 12 hours, 49 minutes. "I love to race," he says. "It's something I want to do for the rest of my life."

Scheidies' Advice: Stay Positive But Realistic

There's a difference between being a realist and being a dreamer, but it's a fine line. As Scheidies' eyesight grew worse, he accepted that he wouldn't be as good an athlete. But he didn't dwell on it--he focused on what he could still do. "I'm a compulsively positive person now," he says.

Ask Ryan Ketchner about pitching, and he talks of his "advantage." He's deaf. "I don't have to deal with pressure from the fans," he explains. "I don't hear them." Ketchner, 24, was born with severe hearing loss, but by his sophomore year of high school, pro scouts were popping up in the stands at John I. Leonard High School in Greenacres, Florida, to see his masterful control and nasty curve. "I was eager to prove everybody wrong," Ketchner says.

Granted, there are challenges, foremost among them communicating with teammates on the field. These days, the obstacle is injury. He was a Southern League All-Star in 2004, but he sat out last season after having surgery on his left elbow. If his rehab goes well, he'll return to baseball this month as a member of the Las Vegas 51s, the Dodgers' triple-A team. From there, he believes he can reach the big leagues. "A lot of people told me I couldn't play baseball," he says. "Had I followed their advice, I wouldn't be where I am today."

He Did This: Played goalie for the Boston Bruins

In Spite Of: Being diagnosed with multiple sclerosis

Two years ago, after tending the net in back-to-back games for Bowling Green University, Jordan Sigalet awoke with numb feet. The next day, he couldn't feel his entire body.

A battery of tests revealed that he had multiple sclerosis. Sigalet, now 25, was prepping for a pro career, so when doctors told him he couldn't play anymore, he took it as a personal challenge. He rejoined his team immediately, numb from the neck down, and was between the pipes for two playoff games. After learning to manage his disease with a drug called Rebif, Sigalet was drafted by the Boston Bruins, joining the club's minor-league affiliate in Providence. Last season, he was called up to Boston for seven games; he played in one and didn't give up a goal. Off the ice, Sigalet is raising awareness about his disease. "Telling my story lifts people's spirits," he says. "And that helps me out a lot, too."

Sigalet's Advice: Become An Expert.

You have to be your own advocate, so learn as much as you can about your personal hurdle. "I did a ton of research on my own to figure out which treatment would work best for me," says Sigalet.

He Did This: Led the Grand Rapids Rampage to a championship

In Spite Of: A fractured neck and paralysis from the waist down

In the final game of the 2000 Arena 2 football season, JoJo Polk, then a 22-year-old rookie cornerback for the Tulsa Talons, sprinted toward the sidewall to break up a pass. He collided at full speed with the plywood wall, snapping his C-6 vertebra. As players and coaches surrounded his inert body, someone asked him if he could move his legs. "I am moving my legs!" he cried out.

The collision left him paralyzed from the waist down. Doctors explained that he'd probably never walk again. "I cried like a baby," Polk recalls. "And after I'd gotten that out of the way, I tried to figure out what I was going to do next." Surgeons agreed not to fuse the bones in Polk's neck (standard procedure), because the operation would reduce his range of motion if he regained the use of his limbs. Feeling slowly began to return. Within weeks, he was taking small steps. Six months after the injury, he was back in the weight room. Then, in the spring of 2001, he got a call: "Are you ready?" It was Polk's former coach, now the defensive coordinator of the Grand Rapids Rampage.

Polk not only made the team, but led the Rampage to a league championship, too. "It was an unbelievable ending," Polk says. "You just can't make something like that up."
