From the earliest point that he began talking about the new Watchmen on HBO, Damon Lindelof has made it clear that his series would be exploring our society's present day relationship with white supremacy. And in the early episodes of his show, he's stuck to that promise, as a mysterious, white supremacist terrorist group called the Seventh Kavalry (note the 'K' spelling of the word 'Cavalry') has figured in as the main threat to the show's protagonists.

The most confusing thing about the Seventh Kavalry is the clothing and costumes that the group tends to wear. Anyone even slightly familiar with the classic graphic novel or even Zack Snyder's 2009 film would recognize the mask of Rorschach, the hyper-moral hero of the original story. But if he's the hero, why are the bad guys dressed in his image?

A major part of this comes from what Lindelof says is a theme within the show: the idea of appropriation. As readers of the graphic novel might recall, Rorschach sent his journal to The New Frontiersman, a conservative-leaning publication (run by Roger Ailes in the show's universe ). It would make sense, then, that his writings could've been misconstrued through the years as representative of that type of ideology; throughout the book Rorschach's journal entries consistently seem to align with what would be considered libertarian views today.

“The idea that the Seventh Cavalry, who seem to be presenting a white supremacist ideology in the pilot, have appropriated Rorschach based on his writings, as a white supremacist," Lindelof said in July at a Television Critics Association event. "Rorschach is dead, he’s not around to basically say, ‘You got it all wrong.'”

By the time the first episode of the HBO show starts up, it sounds like the Kavalry had been a problem in the past, but that things had settled down for a few years. Chief Crawford references something "starting up" again when the officer is shot in the first episode, and we hear allusions to the White Night (which episode 2 gives an extended flashback to).

That 'White Night,' as it turns out, was part of a ripple effect that trickled down all the way from the 1921 Tulsa Riots that kicked off the show. The show's frequent references to Redfordations are alluding to the monetary payments that President Robert Redford made available for victims and ancestors of victims of terrible, racially-motivated acts of violence like that one.

Seventh Kavalry members, against those sorts of payments, tracked down the names and addresses of police officers, and attempted to take them out in one night—the 'White Night.' It was this that led to the Defense of Police Act (DoPA), which in turn meant police identites being hidden, including the wearing of masks. It's all a big, very messed up circle.

Before the show debuted, Lindelof made clear why the show was looking to examine the history and present day presence of white supremacist groups. "To me,Watchmen is a story about America. And it’s about self-proclaimed “heroes” fighting an intangible enemy that is almost impossible to defeat," he wrote on Instagram. "In the eighties, that enemy was the pervasive threat of nuclear Armageddon between the U.S. and the Soviet Union. In 2019, that enemy is the long overdue reckoning with our country’s camouflaged history of White Supremacy."

The first episode alone shows a man with a Rorschach mask in his front seat gun down a police officer, and more members of his organization get in an enormous ranch-set shootout with Angela, Judd, and company. When Looking Glass takes to The Pod, his questions center on involvement with '7k': "Are you a member of, or do you associate with members of, the white supremacist organization known as the Seventh Kavalry?" he asks repeatedly, before a question about taxes—an allusion to the Redfordations that 7K members would be against.

As the show continues, surely more will be explained about the Seventh Kavalry, what they stand for, and what their motivation is. Maybe, as one theory guessed, they were the target of a false flag attack (a la the end of the graphic novel), or Chief Crawford was actually working with them. Or maybe they're representative of the racial divide that still exists among our society to this very day. How Lindelof continues to present this angle in the coming weeks and months is an extremely interesting thing to monitor, and the social angles at play in Watchmen are part of what's made it so interesting to watch in the earlygoings.
