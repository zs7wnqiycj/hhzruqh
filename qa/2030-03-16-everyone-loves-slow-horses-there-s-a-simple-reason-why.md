# Everyone Loves ‘Slow Horses.’ There’s a Simple Reason Why.

[Everyone Loves Slow Horses](https://musclemend.sunsimiaosd.com/)

IN THE FIFTH season of Apple TV+’s Slow Horses, the show’s ragtag spies find themselves in another tight jam. This time, the gang is locked down in their own dumpy headquarters, held hostage by a pair of Regent’s Park agents who’ve stripped away their guns, blocked the exits, and barred them from meddling in a fresh terrorist case. The hairy circumstance leads Jackson Lamb (Gary Oldman), the group’s unheralded leader, to call an audible. Slouched in his chair, he spins a Cold War yarn about a gruesome interrogation that doubles as a secret tutorial. Within the story is a blueprint for their escape—a way for his band of misfits to weaponize the very objects populating Slough House.

To his surprise, the team deciphers his coded anecdote. They turn an aerosol can and lighter into a temporary flamethrower, use a fire extinguisher as a bludgeoning device, and quickly handcuff the discombobulated MI5 guards. Lamb just sits there—half-stunned, half-proud. These were the colleagues he’d written off as “fucking useless,” the ones who once defined “the lowest point in a disappointing career.” Yet here they were, finally listening to him. Maybe they’d been exiled to pasture, but the instincts that got them here, crooked and unconventional as they are, haven’t dulled. “You’ll be thrown out for this,” one of the tied-up agents yells at them. Lamb replies with a smirk: “We’ve already been thrown out.”

That kind of cheeky problem-solving and witty badinage has come to epitomize showrunner Will Smith’s lean and sharply-crafted spy thriller. Since it debuted in 2022, the Emmy-winning series—about a grizzled Cold War spy in charge of a loveably dysfunctional family of rejected MI5 agents tasked with pursuing menial tasks for British Intelligence—has become one of the most digestible and reliable shows within the current television landscape. Not quite an hour-long prestige cable offering, nor a half-hour network procedural, Slow Horses occupies a smart and slick middle ground: six 45-minute episodes wrapped in fully-contained seasons—each filled with twisty, relevant plots, black humor, and kinetic action sequences throughout London’s maze of pubs and back alleys.

That’s a particularly distinct and refreshing niche to carve out right now. Across premium networks and streamers, shows like Stranger Things, Severance, and House of the Dragon have taken on the scope of the blockbuster movie: big budgets, big stars, and big gaps between new seasons. Slow Horses works like a tonic to that bloat, reliably returning each fall (and putting future seasons in the hopper) with a consistently game cast and coinciding with the afterglow of its September awards haul. These days, you don’t need much more publicity than that. As Smith told me in an interview last year, “we've just sort of done it and people have responded—and now, they’re catching up.”

Of course, Slow Horses has warranted praise and critical acclaim for more than its consistency and concision. Among the show’s many pleasures, it’s Oldman who provides its caustic center of gravity. The Oscar-winning actor seamlessly slips into Lamb’s loathsome and confrontational leadership style, turning a greasy curmudgeon into an art form. A respected Cold War agent in the mold of James Bond or George Smiley (whom Oldman portrayed in 2011’s Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy), Lamb has let himself go—he sports long hair, carries a big belly, and struggles to manage his acid reflux and flatulence, which he exacerbates with spicy foods and plenty of liquor. (In the latest season, he lets out a fart so vicious and fertile, it might as well be considered a chemical weapon.) But his slovenly style belies his “Spy-Q.” Lamb still knows the board, the players, and the pieces, preparing him for inevitable chess matches with Diana Taverner (Kristin Scott Thomas), who runs things at Regent’s Park, or the terror organizations they’re trying to thwart.

Lamb can’t do it alone, though. Despite mocking and undressing his stable of horses with F-bombs and eyerolls, he just as quickly leans on their limited skillsets and commends them (when necessary) with endearing sarcasm. Though River Cartwright (Jack Lowden) might be the most notable protagonist, the show’s greatest strength is the way it covers the full spectrum of its diverse cast. Not just a plot device or easy punchline, each Slough House colleague has room to explore the nuances of their specific quirks—whether that’s computer whiz Roddy Ho (Christopher Chung) hacking into databases while getting catfished by internet girlfriends, or Shirley Dander (Aimee-Ffion Edwards) tailing marks while competing with a nasty drug habit. Smith builds a thorough family unit—and a small but growing network of antagonists—out of brief, coarse, sweet moments, all without ever letting them become impervious to the tragic realities of their profession. In this line of work, returning home each night is never guaranteed.

That kind of unpredictability is baked into the show’s structure and narrative, which Smith has mixed up each season to spotlight different returning cast members and ensure new guest stars. The asymmetrical approach is a testament to Mick Herron, the writer of the Slough House novels upon which the show is based. His work has been likened to that of Evelyn Waugh, John Le Carré, and Raymond Chandler, and his novels offer plenty of contemporary, real-world stakes and storylines. In the London of Slow Horses, the city is teeming with filth, shady deals, and ambitious government officials, just enough of a mirror to ground and interrogate its otherwise entertaining puzzle boxes. Those have included plot lines surrounding white nationalists, bureaucratic corruption, child armies, and eerily, this season, a political assassination and mass shooting arc. They’re meaty plots—and the margins are filled with comically acerbic repartee and insider vocabulary—but there’s no fat attached. Smith knows six episodes is the sweet spot: lean enough to keep momentum, long enough to tie every thread.

After all, balance is the key. In the midst of heightened politics and charged rhetoric, Slow Horses has the qualities of a warm cup of tea at the end of a long day. Its characters know the world is a bleak mess. They succumb to its toxicity. They trade insults that sting. They screw up in ways that make things worse. But underneath the callousness, the cynicism, and the insecurities—the traits that provide the show its humor and heaviness—there’s something softer. A stubborn sense of duty. A flicker of hope that their work might still matter. A faint tenderness that hides behind their filthy facades and reaffirms why we care for them. As Lamb once described his team, “I think they’re a bunch of absolute losers. But they’re my losers.” Who can argue with that logic?
