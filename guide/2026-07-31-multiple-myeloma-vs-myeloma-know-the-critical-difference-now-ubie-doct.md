Is multiple myeloma the same as myeloma, or is there a difference?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Multiple myeloma and myeloma are two names for the same disease: a cancer that develops in plasma cells within the bone marrow. Plasma cells are white blood cells that produce antibodies to fight infection. When these cells become cancerous, they multiply uncontrollably, crowd out healthy blood cells, and can damage bones, kidneys, and the immune system.

Explanation

Multiple myeloma, often simply called myeloma, is a cancer that starts in plasma cells. Plasma cells are a type of white blood cell found in the bone marrow, and they help the body fight infections by making antibodies. In myeloma, these plasma cells become cancerous and multiply uncontrollably.

Key Points:

Plasma Cells: These are normally found in the bone marrow and are important for immune function.

Cancerous Growth: In multiple myeloma, the plasma cells grow too much and form tumors in the bone marrow, which can cause bone damage and other health problems.
